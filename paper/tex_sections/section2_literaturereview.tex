\section{Literature Review}

The literature on regression influence has developed from observation-level diagnostics toward methods that examine joint perturbations, robust estimation, and worst-case data deletion. This progression reflects a central econometric concern: empirical conclusions may depend on a small influential subset of observations whose collective effect remains concealed under individual case deletion. Classical diagnostics provide the starting point by measuring residual unusualness, leverage, and coefficient sensitivity at the observation level. Research on masking and joint influence influence then establishes the need for subset-level analysis, while robust regression offers a complementary framework for stabilising estimation under contamination. Recent work on influential sets extends this literature by identifying influential subsets that maximise changes in a target coefficient. These developments motivate two further inferential questions concerning the statistical significance of the largest detected influence and the uncertainty surrounding its effective influential subset size. The review begins with the classical diagnostic framework that first formalised observation-level influence in linear regression.

\subsection{Classical influence diagnostics}
\label{subsec:classical_influence_diagnostics}

Influence analysis examines how individual observations affect fitted regression relationships and the empirical conclusions drawn from them. Residual outliers, leverage points, and influential observations describe related but distinct properties of the sample. A residual outlier has an unusually large model residual, whereas a leverage point occupies an unusual position in the covariate space and can exert substantial control over the fitted regression surface. Influence concerns the realised change in an estimated quantity. The distinction matters because an observation can display one form of unusualness without displaying the others. Classical observation-level diagnostics formalised these relationships by linking unusual data configurations to changes in fitted values, parameter estimates, and inferential summaries \parencite{hampel1974influence,chatterjee1986influential}.

Case deletion became the central operational device for measuring observation-level influence in linear regression \parencite{cook2000detection,cook1979influential}. The procedure removes one observation and compares the refitted model with the full-sample estimate. This comparison creates an interpretable counterfactual in which the observation's contribution is assessed while the specification and the remaining sample are held fixed. Cook's distance condenses the resulting parameter movement into a scalar measure relative to the estimated covariance structure of the coefficient vector. Its common scale supports rapid screening across observations. The measure remains an aggregate diagnostic, so its value must be interpreted alongside diagnostics that isolate covariate geometry and target-specific coefficient changes.

Leverage and DFBETAS provide more specific information about the source and direction of regression sensitivity. Leverage is measured by the diagonal elements of the hat matrix, which quantify how strongly each observation contributes to its own fitted value \parencite{hoaglin1978hat}. A high-leverage observation lies in a covariate region with limited local support, giving its outcome greater potential to rotate or translate the fitted regression surface. High leverage creates the potential for influence, although the realised effect also depends on the observation's outcome and residual. DFBETAS reports the standardised change in a selected coefficient after deleting one observation. This coefficient-specific construction connects the diagnostic directly to the parameter that carries the substantive econometric interpretation \parencite{chatterjee1986influential}.

Influence-function analysis provides a broader conceptual account of deletion diagnostics by studying the sensitivity of an estimator to small perturbations in the empirical distribution \parencite{hampel1974influence,cook1980characterizations}. The empirical influence function translates this local sensitivity into case-specific diagnostics for a fitted sample. This framework clarifies that residual magnitude, leverage, and influence answer different questions: residuals describe disagreement with the fitted conditional mean, leverage describes covariate unusualness, and influence describes the effect of an observation on an estimated quantity. An observation may therefore be unusual without materially affecting the target coefficient. A second observation may have a modest residual and still exert substantial influence because its covariate position gives that residual considerable inferential weight. Reliable diagnosis consequently requires the joint interpretation of the fitted model, the covariate geometry, and the coefficient-specific deletion response \parencite{cook1980characterizations,chatterjee1986influential}.

Observation-level diagnostics remain attractive because they are interpretable, computationally inexpensive, and readily available after ordinary least squares estimation. They provide a useful first audit of empirical sensitivity. Their perturbation design nevertheless evaluates one deletion at a time, preserving every other observation while the diagnostic effect is calculated. This local structure is informative when influential behaviour is concentrated in a single case. When several observations jointly support a distorted fitted relationship, deleting one member leaves the remaining members in place and can produce only a small measured coefficient shift \parencite{chatterjee1986influential}. A group can consequently appear weak under individual deletion while remaining strongly influential under joint deletion, which directs the literature from observation-level diagnostics toward joint influence influence, masking, and swamping.

\subsection{Joint influence influence, masking, and swamping}
\label{subsec:joint_influence}

Joint influence influence arises when the effect of a group of observations cannot be understood from the separate deletion of its individual members. The relevant unit of analysis is therefore an influential influential subset. Observations within such a influential subset may occupy related regions of the covariate space, support a common fitted relationship, or reinforce the same coefficient shift. Their joint deletion can produce a substantial change in the target coefficient even when every leave-one-out diagnostic remains moderate. Early research recognised that regression influence could be distributed across several observations and developed procedures for examining deletions beyond a single case \parencite{andrews1978finding,chatterjee1986influential}. This broader perspective reveals a central limitation of observation-level diagnostics: local deletion effects need not represent the sensitivity created by coordinated observations.

Masking is the principal mechanism through which an influential influential subset escapes individual detection. Masking occurs when several unusual observations support one another so that deleting any single member leaves the distorted regression relationship largely intact \parencite{atkinson1986masking,rousseeuw1990unmasking}. The remaining members continue to anchor the fitted surface, which reduces the residual or coefficient change attributed to the deleted observation. Each member can consequently appear unremarkable under Cook's distance or DFBETAS even though deleting the full influential subset produces a large coefficient shift. The problem becomes especially severe when the observations combine unusual covariate positions with outcomes that follow a common alternative relationship. Masking therefore reflects a structural interaction among observations, and its detection requires an analysis that permits their influence to emerge jointly.

Swamping describes the complementary failure in which regular observations are incorrectly identified as influential because an unusual influential subset has distorted the diagnostic baseline. The fitted regression surface, residual scale, and covariance structure are all estimated from the contaminated sample. An influential influential subset can move these quantities sufficiently far from the dominant data pattern that well-behaved observations acquire large residuals or deletion measures \parencite{rousseeuw1990unmasking,hadi1993procedures}. The resulting diagnostic ranking directs attention away from the observations that created the distortion. Swamping and masking can therefore occur together: the influential influential subset receives weak diagnostic scores, and regular observations receive strong scores. This interaction limits the reliability of threshold-based screening when the underlying problem involves several mutually reinforcing cases.

Joint influence deletion procedures were developed to identify these concealed structures more directly. \textcite{andrews1978finding} examined subsets of observations whose removal materially changes regression results, establishing an early foundation for influential-subset analysis. Subsequent methods used robust distances, ordered residuals, and sequential subset construction to separate multiple outliers and leverage points from the remaining sample \parencite{rousseeuw1990unmasking,hadi1992identifying,hadi1993procedures}. Influence-matrix methods provided another route by representing relationships among case-deletion effects and searching for groups with coordinated influence \parencite{pena1995detection}. These procedures expanded the diagnostic unit from an observation to a subset. Their contributions established that joint influence is a distinct empirical phenomenon, although the selected subsets often depend on screening rules, starting values, or sequential inclusion decisions.

Subset-level analysis creates a substantial computational problem because the number of possible influential subsets increases rapidly with the sample size. For a prespecified influential subset size \(k\), an exhaustive procedure must consider \(\binom{n}{k}\) candidate deletions. The search becomes infeasible even in moderately sized datasets when several influential subset sizes are examined. Sequential procedures and influence-based approximations reduce this burden by prioritising observations or subsets that appear promising under local criteria \parencite{pena2005new,zhao2019multiple}. These reductions improve computational feasibility. They can also preserve the masking problem when the preliminary ranking is derived from individual or approximate influence measures, since a jointly influential influential subset may contain observations that appear weak before they are evaluated together \parencite{kuschnig2021hidden}.

Modern influential-set analysis converts the diagnostic problem into an explicit optimisation problem over candidate influential subsets. The objective is defined by the empirical conclusion under examination, such as the absolute change in a target coefficient after deleting a subset of a prespecified size \parencite{kuschnig2021hidden}. This formulation avoids dependence on a universal definition of outlyingness because the selected influential subset is tied directly to the coefficient being audited. It also makes the computational requirement transparent: the procedure must identify the deletion set that produces the largest coefficient shift among all sets of the same size. Influential subset size remains fixed at this stage, so the analysis does not yet determine which value of \(k\) receives the strongest empirical support. The central question consequently changes from ``Which observation is influential?'' to ``Which subset of size \(k\) produces the largest change in the target coefficient?''

\subsection{Robust regression and its distinct objective}
\label{subsec:robust_regression}

Robust regression developed in response to the instability of classical estimators when a sample contains outliers, high-leverage observations, or departures from the assumed error distribution. Ordinary least squares assigns quadratic loss to residuals, so observations with large residuals can contribute disproportionately to the fitted coefficients. Robust procedures modify the estimation criterion to limit this sensitivity and preserve reliable estimation under moderate contamination. Their central objective is stability across plausible deviations from the statistical model. This objective provides an important benchmark for evaluating influence diagnostics because both literatures address sensitivity to unusual observations from different analytical perspectives.

The influence function supplied an early formal framework for describing the local robustness of an estimator. It measures the effect of an infinitesimal amount of contamination placed at a particular point in the population distribution \parencite{hampel1974influence}. An unbounded influence function indicates that sufficiently extreme observations can have arbitrarily large effects on the estimator. Huber's M-estimation framework controls this local sensitivity by replacing squared residual loss with a function whose marginal contribution grows more slowly in the tails \parencite{huber1964location,huber1973robust}. The resulting estimators retain much of the efficiency of least squares under approximately Gaussian errors while reducing the effect of observations with large residuals. Bounded influence therefore establishes local resistance, although it does not by itself guarantee protection against a substantial proportion of contamination.

The breakdown point evaluates robustness under larger perturbations by measuring the smallest fraction of contamination capable of driving an estimator arbitrarily far from the underlying parameter. Least squares has a low breakdown point because a single sufficiently extreme observation can severely distort the fitted model. High-breakdown procedures were developed to preserve bounded estimates when a larger share of the sample is contaminated. This criterion extends robustness beyond local influence and evaluates the estimator's resistance to adverse data configurations. A high breakdown point remains an estimator-level property. It does not directly identify which observations produce the largest change in a specific coefficient.

High-breakdown regression methods implement this objective through alternative fitting criteria. Least Median of Squares minimises the median squared residual and can therefore tolerate substantial contamination \parencite{rousseeuw1984least}. Least Trimmed Squares applies a related principle by fitting the model to a selected proportion of the smallest squared residuals, which reduces the contribution of observations that are poorly aligned with the dominant regression pattern \parencite{rousseeuw2011robust}. Scale-based estimators extend this approach by choosing coefficients that minimise a robust measure of residual dispersion \parencite{yohai1988high}. MM-estimation combines a high-breakdown initial estimate with a subsequent M-estimation step designed to improve statistical efficiency \parencite{yohai1987high}. These procedures balance resistance and efficiency through different loss functions, scale estimates, and tuning constants.

Robust fitting can reduce the visibility of the observations that created the original instability because down-weighting and trimming are primarily directed toward producing a stable coefficient estimate. The final estimate may therefore be reliable while providing limited information about the separate effects of individual observations or influential subsets. Monitoring procedures address this limitation by tracing how estimates, residuals, and diagnostic quantities evolve as observations are progressively introduced into the fitted sample \parencite{riani2014monitoring}. This process preserves more information about the relationship between the observations and the resulting estimate. Monitoring remains connected to the robust fitting objective, since the ordering and diagnostic path are organised around the construction of a resistant fit. A coefficient-specific influence audit requires a different optimisation target.

Robust regression and influential-subset analysis consequently answer distinct empirical questions. Robust regression seeks stable estimation under contamination, and its primary output is a resistant estimate of the regression parameter. Influential-subset analysis identifies the group of observations whose deletion produces the largest change in a prespecified target coefficient. The two approaches are complementary because a robust estimator can assess whether the substantive estimate persists under resistant fitting, while an influential-subset procedure can reveal the data structure responsible for coefficient sensitivity. Neither objective supplies the other's principal output. This distinction motivates the modern literature on worst-case data dropping, where the search is explicitly organised around the largest attainable change in a target conclusion.

\subsection{Worst-case data dropping and most influential sets}
\label{subsec:worst_case_data_dropping}

\subsubsection{Approximate data influence and data-dropping sensitivity}
\label{subsubsec:approximate_data_influence}

Modern data-dropping analysis extends classical influence diagnostics by asking how an empirical conclusion changes when selected observations are removed from the estimation sample. This question places the target coefficient or prediction at the centre of the analysis. Influence-function approximations offer an efficient starting point because they estimate the effect of removing an observation through a local expansion around the full-sample estimator. In machine learning, \textcite{koh2017understanding} use influence functions to approximate how individual training observations affect fitted predictions without repeatedly estimating the model. Related theoretical work studies the conditions under which such approximations remain accurate for smooth estimation problems \parencite{fisher2023influence}. These methods improve computational feasibility, although their local construction may provide an incomplete representation of large or interacting deletions.

Group influence methods extend the perturbation from one observation to a subset. The extension is important because interactions within a subset can create an aggregate effect that differs from the sum of its individual effects. First-order group influence approximations often combine observation-level contributions, while second-order methods incorporate curvature and interactions among selected observations \parencite{koh2019accuracy,basu2020second}. Data valuation methods approach the same broad problem by assigning each observation a contribution to predictive performance or another model-based objective \parencite{ghorbani2019data}. Infinitesimal-jackknife methods similarly estimate the consequences of changing observation weights without complete model refitting \parencite{sattigeri2022fair}. These approaches provide scalable measures of data importance, but the resulting ranking need not identify the subset that maximises the change in a target coefficient.

Finite-sample robustness analysis converts data importance into a more direct sensitivity question: how much data must be removed before a reported conclusion changes materially? This formulation permits substantive events such as a sign reversal, loss of statistical significance, or coefficient movement beyond a prespecified threshold. \textcite{giordano2026automatic1, giordano2026automatic2} develop a finite-sample robustness metric based on the smallest fraction of observations capable of changing a conclusion, together with computational approximations for identifying damaging deletions. Related work applies small-data-removal sensitivity to analyses whose estimators require computationally intensive procedures, including Markov chain Monte Carlo \parencite{nguyen2024sensitivity}. This literature establishes worst-case data dropping as a general robustness problem, while leaving the quality of the selected subset dependent on the search method.

\subsubsection{Computational robustness auditing}
\label{subsubsec:computational_robustness_auditing}

Worst-case data dropping creates a combinatorial optimisation problem because the analyst must search across many possible subsets. For a sample of size \(n\), there are \(\binom{n}{k}\) candidate deletion sets of size \(k\). Direct enumeration therefore becomes infeasible as either the sample size or the permitted deletion size increases. Complexity results show that robust and sparse optimisation problems can remain computationally difficult even when their objectives have relatively simple statistical interpretations \parencite{price2022hardness}. Robustness-auditing methods respond with relaxations, greedy searches, local approximations, and problem-specific algorithms \parencite{suriyakumar2022algorithms,moitra2023provably}. These methods make large-scale auditing possible, although computational tractability and exact subset recovery remain separate properties.

Recent auditing research examines how closely efficient algorithms approximate the most damaging deletion set. \textcite{freund2023practical} develop practical methods for auditing ordinary least squares and study the computational challenges associated with identifying small subsets that substantially alter regression conclusions. \textcite{hu2024most} formulate most influential subset selection as a broader optimisation problem and document the opportunities and difficulties created by interactions among observations. Later work extends regression auditing to settings near singularity, where deletion can substantially alter the conditioning and effective rank of the design matrix \parencite{rubinstein2025robustness}. These contributions show that sensitivity can arise through both coefficient movement and changes in the geometry of the regression problem. An auditing procedure must therefore account for the statistical objective and the numerical structure induced by deletion.

Approximation quality becomes especially important when the selected subset is interpreted as an empirical explanation. A local approximation may accurately estimate small perturbations while ranking larger deletion sets incorrectly. Interactions among observations, masking, curvature, and denominator instability can all weaken the correspondence between approximate and realised coefficient shifts. \textcite{huang2025approximations} identify failure modes in approximations to worst-case data dropping and show that apparently accurate local measures can miss damaging subsets under relevant data configurations. The resulting problem concerns more than the estimated magnitude of sensitivity. When researchers attach substantive meaning to the identities of the selected observations, an inaccurate subset can produce an inaccurate structural interpretation even when it signals that some sensitivity exists.

\subsubsection{Exact most influential sets}
\label{subsubsec:exact_most_influential_sets}

Most Influential Sets provide a coefficient-targeted formulation of exact subset influence in linear models. \textcite{kuschnig2021hidden} show that groups of observations can remain concealed under conventional diagnostics while jointly determining the magnitude or significance of an estimated coefficient. Their framework searches directly for the subset that produces the largest change in a specified regression quantity. This formulation addresses masking because observations are evaluated as members of a set rather than through a preliminary observation-level ranking. Subsequent work develops algorithms for finding most influential sets through the structure of the linear regression deletion problem \parencite{kuschnig2026finding}. Exact search gives the selected observations a clear interpretation: among all admissible subsets of the same size, they generate the largest realised shift in the target coefficient.

For a prespecified subset size \(k\), the fixed-\(k\) MIS objective can be represented conceptually as
\[
S_k^*
\in
\arg\max_{\substack{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\ |S|=k}}
\left|
\widehat{\beta}_{j,-S}
-
\widehat{\beta}_{j}
\right|,
\]
where \(\widehat{\beta}_{j}\) is the full-sample estimate of the target coefficient and \(\widehat{\beta}_{j,-S}\) is its estimate after deleting \(S\). The absolute value permits influential subsets that move the coefficient in either direction. The objective is conditional on \(k\), so it identifies the most influential subset for a chosen deletion size. It does not determine whether that size is empirically distinguished from nearby alternatives. The full reduction of this objective, its linear-fractional representation, and the exact search algorithm belong in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.

Exact fixed-\(k\) search resolves the subset-identification problem for each admissible size, but it leaves several inferential questions open. A large observed coefficient shift may reflect unusual structural sensitivity or the ordinary extreme behaviour generated by searching across many subsets. A multiscale analysis also searches across several values of \(k\), creating uncertainty about the effective subset size. Positive and negative shifts may have different empirical support, while deletions near singular designs can create denominator-safety concerns. The identities of the selected observations and the local influence profile may also change sharply across neighbouring sizes. These unresolved questions concern statistical significance, scale selection, direction, denominator safety, and local stability, motivating the inferential procedures developed in the following subsections.


\subsection{Statistical inference after multiscale subset search}
\label{subsec:inference_after_subset_search}

Exact MIS search identifies the subset that produces the largest change in a target coefficient for a prespecified subset size. The magnitude of this change cannot be interpreted through conventional inference because the reported value is selected as the maximum over many candidate subsets. A multiscale analysis expands the search across several values of \(k\), while a bidirectional analysis considers both positive and negative coefficient shifts. The observed result therefore reflects selection over subset membership, subset size, and direction. This search creates multiple opportunities to obtain a large statistic under ordinary sampling variation. Statistical inference must reproduce these opportunities when constructing the relevant null distribution.

Existing work on testing most influential sets develops an Extreme Value Theory approach to this problem \parencite{konrad2026testing}. Extreme Value Theory provides a framework for modelling the limiting behaviour of maxima and other rare upper-tail events \parencite{fisher1928limiting,gnedenko1943distribution}. Its applied methods include block-maxima models and threshold-exceedance models for approximating the distribution of extreme observations \parencite{davison1990models,bucher2017maximum}. In the MIS setting, the maximum influence statistic is treated as an extreme value generated by a large collection of candidate subsets. This approach supplies a formal test of whether the observed maximum is unusually large. Its performance depends on the appropriateness of the tail approximation and the treatment of dependence among overlapping subsets.

Residual permutation provides a distinct route to null calibration in regression models. The procedure estimates the model under the null hypothesis, separates the fitted component from the residual variation, and constructs pseudo-outcomes by permuting the residuals. Each pseudo-outcome preserves the systematic component imposed by the null while altering the allocation of residual variation across observations. Residual-permutation methods have been developed for regression and the general linear model under conditions supporting the required exchangeability or invariance argument \parencite{ter1992permutation,winkler2014permutation,wen2025residual}. Recent work provides finite-sample validity for a specially constructed residual permutation test under fixed design and exchangeable errors, while also showing that direct permutation of fitted regression residuals need not inherit this validity \parencite{wen2025residual} These conditions define the scope of the test. Residual permutation does not provide unrestricted validity under arbitrary heteroskedasticity or dependence.

Random-permutation theory permits the null distribution to be approximated using a finite collection of randomly generated transformations. Complete enumeration is generally infeasible because each permutation requires another multiscale MIS search. \textcite{hemerik2018exact} establish conditions under which tests based on randomly sampled permutations retain finite-sample validity when the observed data arrangement is included appropriately. This result supports computationally feasible permutation inference without treating the simulated null sample as an independent approximation unrelated to the observed arrangement. The number of random permutations still determines the resolution of the resulting \(p\)-value. A sufficiently large permutation budget is therefore required to support rejection at the chosen significance level.

Simultaneous adjustment is necessary because MIS-SAP reports the largest statistic obtained across several subset sizes and two coefficient-shift directions.  The maximum statistic is calculated across the entire search family for the observed sample and recalculated across the same family within every null resample. This construction accounts for the selection opportunities created by overlapping subsets and neighbouring values of \(k\). Multiscale testing further shows that statistics evaluated at different scales can have different null behaviour and may require scale-dependent calibration \parencite{dumbgen2001multiscale,walther2010optimal,walther2022calibrating}. This literature motivates comparing the observed influence at each \(k\) with a \(k\)-specific null benchmark; the exact normalized-exceedance selector used by MIS-SAP is a thesis-defined design choice and is formalised in the methodology section. Post-selection inference provides the related principle that uncertainty statements must account for the data-dependent process that selected the reported result \parencite{berk2013valid}.

Monte Carlo permutation inference also requires an appropriate treatment of finite simulation error. A raw exceedance proportion can equal zero when none of the simulated maximum statistics exceeds the observed maximum. Such a value overstates the information supplied by a finite number of random permutations. \textcite{phipson2010permutation} show that permutation \(p\)-values based on randomly drawn permutations should include a correction that incorporates the observed arrangement and prevents zero estimates. The corrected value also makes the minimum attainable \(p\)-value explicit. The simulation budget must consequently be chosen so that this minimum lies below the intended significance level.

Sequential Monte Carlo methods can reduce the computational cost of permutation inference when the accumulating evidence permits an early decision. This feature is especially relevant for MIS-SAP because each additional permutation repeats the exact search across all admissible subset sizes and directions. \textcite{besag1991sequential} provide a foundation for sequential Monte Carlo \(p\)-values based on prespecified stopping rules. A valid stopping rule depends on the observed exceedance process and must be defined before examining the simulated results. Informal termination after the estimated \(p\)-value appears stable can alter the test's error properties. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} defines the stopping boundary used by MIS-SAP.

MIS-SAP integrates these components into a selection-adjusted permutation test for influential-subset detection. Existing MIS testing provides an EVT-based route for evaluating maximum influence \parencite{konrad2026testing}. MIS-SAP constructs a residual-permutation route that repeats the complete multiscale and bidirectional search within every null resample. The resulting global test evaluates whether the largest observed coefficient shift is unusual relative to the maxima produced under the null search process. Rejection indicates evidence of an unusually influential subset within the examined range of \(k\). It does not determine whether one subset size is uniquely supported or whether the selected observations remain stable across nearby sizes. These questions require a separate assessment of uncertainty about the effective influential-subset size.

\subsection{Uncertainty about the effective influential-subset size}
\label{subsec:effective_subset_size}

A statistically significant global MIS result establishes that excessive influence exists within the examined range of subset sizes, but it does not establish that one value of \(k\) is uniquely supported. The estimated influence profile may be flat, noisy, or irregular across neighbouring subset sizes. In such settings, the empirical maximiser can change after a small perturbation even when the broader profile remains almost unchanged. Reporting only the maximising value creates an appearance of precision that the data may not support. Inference about the effective influential-subset size should therefore distinguish a clearly identified optimum from a collection of statistically plausible sizes.

Set identification provides a natural framework for representing this uncertainty. Partially identified models replace a single parameter estimate with a set of values that remain compatible with the available information and maintained assumptions \parencite{chernozhukov2007estimation}. Related work on intersection bounds develops simultaneous procedures for extrema formed from several estimated inequalities or criterion values \parencite{chernozhukov2013intersection}. Simultaneous uncertainty must therefore be considered when comparing the directional profiles. Results on maxima and suprema motivate using a common simultaneous error bound when several estimated profile values are compared \parencite{chernozhukov2013gaussian,chernozhukov2014gaussian}. MIS-sek adapts this principle to determine whether the two influence directions are sufficiently separated at a given subset size. The exact directional-separation rule defining \(\widehat K_1\) is specific to MIS-sek and is formalised in the methodology section.

Near-optimality alone does not establish that the direction of the coefficient shift is clearly determined. For each subset size, the bidirectional MIS search produces a largest positive shift and a largest negative shift. Similar magnitudes in the two directions indicate that the estimated profile does not strongly distinguish which form of deletion sensitivity dominates. Simultaneous uncertainty must therefore be considered when comparing the directional profiles. MIS-sek retains a value of \(k\) in the directional-separation set \(\widehat K_1\) only when the gap between the two directions is sufficiently large relative to the common uncertainty radius. This condition prevents a weak directional difference from being interpreted as a stable feature of the influential-subset structure.

The reliability of an influence profile also depends on the numerical stability of the deleted regression problem. Exact MIS search for a target coefficient contains a denominator that reflects the residualised variation remaining in the target regressor after deletion \parencite{kuschnig2026finding}. A small denominator indicates that the retained design is approaching singularity or contains insufficient identifying variation for a stable coefficient comparison. Large coefficient shifts generated in this region may reflect numerical amplification as well as substantive influence. MIS-sek therefore restricts the near-optimal and directional comparisons to denominator-admissible subset sizes. Denominator safety is applied before the primary support sets are constructed, ensuring that an unstable candidate cannot enter the certificate solely because it produces an extreme profile value.

Stability of the selected observations supplies a further source of evidence about the interpretation of \(k\). If neighbouring subset sizes describe the same underlying influential structure, their selected subsets should retain substantial membership overlap as observations are added or removed. The overlap-support set \(\widehat K_2\) records the subset sizes that satisfy the thesis-defined neighbouring-set persistence requirement. Stability selection provides conceptual motivation for treating persistence under perturbation as evidence of reliable structure \parencite{meinshausen2010stability}. Its theoretical results concern a different variable-selection problem. They do not establish the specific overlap threshold, neighbouring-set comparison, or support-dilation rule used by MIS-sek, which are components of the methodology developed in this thesis.

A stable effective subset size should also be supported by the local shape of the influence profile. An isolated spike may identify an empirical maximum while remaining highly sensitive to minor changes in the data or search grid. A locally stable profile displays a more persistent pattern across neighbouring values of \(k\), indicating that the selected size is embedded in a coherent region of elevated influence. A related idea appears in adaptive nonparametric estimation, where estimates obtained at different tuning scales are compared while accounting for the uncertainty associated with each scale \parencite{goldenshluger2011bandwidth}. MIS-sek uses this literature as conceptual motivation for requiring local persistence across neighbouring values of \(k\). The exact neighbouring-\(k\) criterion and local-profile threshold defining \(\widehat K_3\) are thesis-defined and do not follow directly from the Goldenshluger--Lepski procedure.

MIS-sek combines the four forms of evidence through a conservative intersection. A subset size enters the effective influential-subset certificate only when it is near-optimal, directionally separated, denominator-safe, supported by neighbouring-set overlap, and consistent with local profile stability. The resulting set may contain one value, several values, or no values. A singleton supports point identification of the effective subset size, while a multi-valued certificate reports the remaining uncertainty directly. An empty intersection indicates that the available diagnostics do not jointly support a stable effective size, even when the preceding global test rejects the null of no excessive influence. The hard intersection is a thesis-defined certification rule that combines these separate sources of evidence. The resulting certificate targets a stable effective subset size. Without additional theory, it does not identify an exact latent contamination count. This hard-intersection construction separates detection from subset-size certification and motivates the formal definitions of \(\widehat K_0\), \(\widehat K_1\), \(\widehat K_2\), \(\widehat K_3\), and \(\widehat K_{\mathrm{sek}}\) in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.
