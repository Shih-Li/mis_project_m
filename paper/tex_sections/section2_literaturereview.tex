\section{Literature Review}

The literature on regression influence now connects observation-level diagnostics, joint influence, robust estimation, worst-case deletion, and statistical inference for maximum influence. These strands address a common econometric problem: a fitted coefficient can depend heavily on a small group of observations even when individual case-deletion measures remain moderate. Classical diagnostics describe observation-level sensitivity, while robust regression studies stable estimation under contamination and related departures from the reference model. Most Influential Sets extend the deletion perspective to coefficient-targeted subset optimisation. The remaining literature then motivates fixed-\(k\) inference for the resulting maximum and an analysis of how that sensitivity behaves under distinct forms of model misspecification.

\subsection{Classical influence diagnostics}
\label{subsec:classical_influence_diagnostics}

Classical influence analysis distinguishes residual unusualness, leverage, and realised influence on an estimated regression quantity. A residual outlier has an unusually large fitted residual, whereas a leverage point occupies an unusual position in the covariate space. Influence concerns the change induced in an estimate or fitted quantity. These properties can occur separately. An observation may have high leverage with little effect on the target coefficient, while another may exert substantial influence through a modest residual at an unusual covariate position. Classical diagnostics formalise these distinctions through case-deletion and perturbation measures \parencite{hampel1974influence,chatterjee1986influential}.

Case deletion provides the basic operational framework for observation-level regression influence \parencite{cook2000detection}. The procedure removes one observation and compares the refitted model with the full-sample estimate. This comparison isolates the realised contribution of that case under a fixed model specification and an otherwise unchanged sample. Cook's distance summarises the resulting coefficient movement relative to the estimated covariance structure. It is an aggregate measure. Its interpretation is therefore strengthened by diagnostics that separately describe covariate geometry and target-specific coefficient movement.

Leverage and DFBETAS identify the source and direction of regression sensitivity. Leverage is measured by the diagonal elements of the hat matrix, which quantify how strongly each observation contributes to its own fitted value \parencite{hoaglin1978hat}. A high-leverage observation lies in a covariate region with limited local support, giving its outcome greater potential to rotate or translate the fitted regression surface. Realised influence still depends on the outcome and residual. DFBETAS reports the standardised change in a selected regression coefficient after deleting one observation from the fitted sample \parencite{chatterjee1986influential}. This coefficient-specific construction links observation-level deletion directly to the parameter carrying the substantive econometric interpretation.

Influence-function analysis places case-deletion diagnostics within a broader perturbation framework \parencite{hampel1974influence,cook1980characterizations}. The influence function studies how an estimator responds to a small change in the underlying empirical distribution, and its sample analogue translates that local sensitivity into case-specific diagnostics. Residuals describe disagreement with the fitted conditional mean, leverage describes covariate unusualness, and influence describes the effect of an observation on an estimated quantity. An observation may be unusual without materially affecting the target coefficient. A second observation may carry a modest residual and still exert substantial influence, because its covariate position gives that residual considerable inferential weight. Reliable diagnosis therefore requires the joint interpretation of the fitted model, the covariate geometry, and the coefficient-specific deletion response \parencite{cook1980characterizations,chatterjee1986influential}.

Observation-level diagnostics remain useful because they are interpretable, inexpensive, and routinely available after ordinary least squares estimation. Their deletion design evaluates one case while every other observation remains in the fitted sample. This structure works well when sensitivity is concentrated in one observation. It can fail under joint influence. When several observations support the same coefficient distortion, deleting one member leaves the remaining configuration intact and produces only a small measured coefficient shift \parencite{chatterjee1986influential}. This limitation moves the analysis from individual cases to masking, swamping, and subset-level influence.

\subsection{Joint influence, masking, and swamping}
\label{subsec:joint_influence}

Joint influence arises when the effect of a group cannot be inferred from separate deletion of its individual members. Observations in such a subset may occupy related covariate regions, support a common fitted relationship, or reinforce the same coefficient shift. Their joint deletion can then produce a substantial target-coefficient change while every leave-one-out measure remains moderate. The subset is the relevant diagnostic unit. Early work recognised that regression influence could be distributed across several observations and developed procedures extending deletion beyond one case \parencite{andrews1978finding,chatterjee1986influential}. This literature establishes the conceptual basis for subset-level influence and raises the question of how such subsets are identified.

Masking occurs when several influential observations support one another strongly enough to escape individual detection \parencite{atkinson1986masking,rousseeuw1990unmasking}. Deleting one member leaves the remaining observations in place, so the fitted relationship can remain close to its original distorted position. The measured case-deletion effect is then reduced. Each member can appear unremarkable under Cook's distance or DFBETAS even though deleting the full subset produces a large coefficient shift. Masking is especially strong when related high-leverage observations support a common alternative relationship in the covariate and outcome space. Its detection therefore requires a procedure that allows influence to emerge jointly.

Swamping occurs when observations outside the influential subset receive large diagnostic values because the fitted baseline has already been distorted. The regression surface, residual scale, and covariance structure are estimated from the observed sample. An influential subset can move these quantities far enough from the dominant data pattern that well-behaved observations acquire large residuals or deletion measures \parencite{rousseeuw1990unmasking,hadi1993procedures}. The ranking then directs attention away from the observations responsible. Masking and swamping can occur together within the same contaminated sample and distort opposite sides of the diagnostic ranking. Their combination limits threshold-based screening when several observations jointly determine the fitted relationship.

Subset-deletion methods were developed to identify these coordinated structures directly. \textcite{andrews1978finding} examined groups whose removal materially changes regression results, providing an early foundation for influential-subset analysis. Later procedures used robust distances, ordered residuals, and sequential subset construction to separate multiple unusual observations from the remaining sample \parencite{rousseeuw1990unmasking,hadi1992identifying,hadi1993procedures}. Influence-matrix methods offered another route by representing relationships among case-deletion effects and searching for groups with coordinated influence \parencite{pena1995detection}. These contributions established joint influence as a distinct empirical phenomenon. The selected subsets nevertheless depend on screening rules, starting values, and sequential inclusion decisions, which leaves the influence of a subset of given size without a direct optimisation criterion.

Subset-level diagnosis creates a combinatorial search problem because the number of candidate deletion sets grows rapidly with \(n\) and \(k\). For a
fixed deletion size \(k\), exhaustive search requires evaluation of \(\binom{n}{k}\) subsets. The search becomes infeasible even in moderately
sized samples. Sequential procedures and local influence approximations reduce the computational burden by prioritising promising candidates under locally defined screening criteria \parencite{pena2005new,zhao2019multiple}. Their preliminary rankings can still inherit observation-level masking, because a member of a jointly influential subset may appear weak before the full subset is evaluated \parencite{kuschnig2021hidden}. Computational feasibility therefore does not guarantee recovery of the maximally influential subset.

Modern influential-set analysis formulates joint deletion as an explicit optimisation problem over subsets. The objective can be defined by the absolute change in a target coefficient after deleting a subset of prespecified size \parencite{kuschnig2021hidden}. This definition ties influence to the empirical parameter under study and removes any reliance on a universal criterion of outlyingness. The computational requirement becomes explicit: under a prespecified deletion budget, the task is to recover the subset that maximises the target-coefficient change.

\subsection{Robust regression and its distinct objective}
\label{subsec:robust_regression}

Robust regression addresses instability by adopting estimation rules that remain stable when observations depart from the reference model. Ordinary least squares assigns quadratic loss to residuals, so large residuals can have substantial effects on fitted coefficients. Robust procedures limit this sensitivity through alternative loss functions, observation weights, or trimming rules applied to the fitted sample. Their primary objective is stable estimation. This objective differs from the task of identifying observations that jointly generate a specified coefficient change. Robust regression therefore provides a complementary benchmark for coefficient-targeted influence analysis.

The influence function supplies a formal framework for local robustness. It measures how an estimator responds when an infinitesimal amount of contamination is placed at a specified point in the underlying distribution \parencite{hampel1974influence}. An unbounded influence function permits extreme observations to have arbitrarily large local effects. Huber's M-estimation framework limits sensitivity in the residual direction by replacing quadratic loss with a slower-growing contribution in the tails \parencite{huber1964location,huber1973robust}. The resulting estimator retains substantial efficiency under Gaussian errors. Its influence function remains unbounded in the covariate direction, so a single high-leverage observation can still dominate the fit.

The breakdown point measures robustness under larger departures by recording the smallest contamination fraction capable of driving an estimator arbitrarily far from its target. The finite-sample breakdown point of least squares is \(1/n\), so its asymptotic breakdown point is zero. High-breakdown procedures raise this fraction by changing the fitting criterion used when contamination affects a substantial share of the sample. The criterion describes global stability under adverse contamination. It does not identify which observations produce the largest movement in a particular coefficient.

High-breakdown regression methods implement this objective through resistant fitting criteria. Least Trimmed Squares minimises the sum of a prespecified number of the smallest squared residuals \parencite{rousseeuw1984applying}. MM-estimation combines a high-breakdown initial estimator with a subsequent M-estimation step designed to improve efficiency while retaining the breakdown point of the initial estimator \parencite{yohai1987high}. These methods provide the robust-estimation benchmarks used in the simulation analysis.

Robust fitting can reduce the visibility of observations that generated the original instability because down-weighting and trimming target the fitted estimate. A resistant coefficient may therefore reveal little about the separate contribution of individual observations or subsets. Monitoring procedures recover part of this information by tracing estimates and diagnostics as observations enter the fitted sample \parencite{riani2014monitoring}. Their organising objective remains resistant fitting. Coefficient-targeted influence instead searches for the subset whose joint deletion produces the largest change in the specified coefficient.

Robust regression and MIS therefore answer distinct empirical questions. Robust regression asks whether stable estimation can be maintained under contamination or related departures from the reference model. MIS asks which observations jointly account for the largest sensitivity of a specified coefficient. A resistant estimate can establish stability, while an influential-set analysis can identify the data configuration responsible for coefficient movement. Neither objective supplies the other's principal output. This distinction leads directly to the literature on worst-case data deletion.

\subsection{Worst-case data dropping and most influential sets}
\label{subsec:worst_case_data_dropping}

\subsubsection{Approximate data influence and data-dropping sensitivity}
\label{subsubsec:approximate_data_influence}

Modern data-dropping analysis studies how a specified empirical conclusion changes when selected observations are removed from the estimation sample. Influence-function methods provide a computationally efficient approximation through a local expansion around the full-sample estimator. \textcite{koh2017understanding} use this construction to approximate how individual training observations affect fitted predictions without repeated model refitting. Related theoretical work establishes conditions under which such approximations remain accurate for smooth estimation problems \parencite{fisher2023influence}. The approximation is local by construction, so larger deletions can produce interactions and curvature that a first-order expansion represents weakly.

Group influence extends local perturbation methods from one observation to a collection of observations. The extension matters because interactions within a group can create an aggregate effect that differs from the sum of the individual effects. First-order procedures aggregate case contributions, while higher-order approximations incorporate interactions and curvature within a selected group \parencite{koh2019accuracy,basu2020second}. These approximations provide scalable measures of group sensitivity. Their rankings can still inherit masking because observations that appear weak under local perturbations may become influential when evaluated jointly.

Finite-sample robustness analysis states the deletion question in terms of a change in the reported conclusion. Relevant changes include sign reversal, loss of statistical significance, or movement beyond a substantively chosen coefficient threshold. \textcite{giordano2026automatic1,giordano2026automatic2} study the smallest fraction of observations capable of changing a conclusion and develop computational approximations for harmful deletions. Related work extends small-data-removal sensitivity to estimators that require computationally intensive procedures, including Markov chain Monte Carlo \parencite{nguyen2024sensitivity}. This literature establishes worst-case data dropping as a general robustness problem. The quality of the reported answer depends on the search method, which makes subset selection part of the substantive assessment.

\subsubsection{Computational robustness auditing}
\label{subsubsec:computational_robustness_auditing}

Worst-case deletion produces a combinatorial optimisation problem because the analyst must search over many candidate subsets. With \(n\) observations and deletion size \(k\), the search space contains \(\binom{n}{k}\) subsets. Direct enumeration soon becomes infeasible. Complexity results show that related robustness and sparse optimisation problems can remain computationally difficult even under simple statistical objectives \parencite{price2022hardness}. Practical auditing methods therefore use relaxations, greedy searches, local approximations, and problem-specific algorithms \parencite{suriyakumar2022algorithms,moitra2022provably}. These methods make large-scale auditing possible, although computational tractability and exact subset recovery remain distinct properties.

Recent auditing research studies how closely efficient algorithms recover the most damaging deletion set. \textcite{freund2023towards} develop practical robustness-auditing methods for ordinary least squares, while \textcite{hu2024most} formulate most influential subset selection as a general optimisation problem and analyse its computational structure. Interactions among observations complicate both approximation and search. Deletion can also move the design toward singularity and alter the effective rank of the regression problem \parencite{rubinstein2025robustness}. Sensitivity therefore arises through coefficient movement and through changes in the geometry of the deleted design. A reliable audit must account for both channels.

Approximation quality becomes especially important when the selected observations receive a substantive interpretation. A local approximation can estimate small perturbations accurately while ranking larger deletion sets incorrectly. Masking, curvature, interaction effects, and near-singular deleted designs can weaken the correspondence between approximate scores and realised coefficient changes. \textcite{huang2025approximations} document failure modes in approximations to worst-case data dropping under relevant data configurations. An approximate procedure may therefore reveal that substantial sensitivity exists without recovering the subset that maximises the realised target-coefficient change. This distinction motivates exact optimisation when the identities of the selected observations form part of the empirical interpretation.

\subsubsection{Exact most influential sets}
\label{subsubsec:exact_most_influential_sets}

Most Influential Sets provide a coefficient-targeted formulation of joint deletion in linear models. \textcite{kuschnig2021hidden} show that groups of observations can remain concealed under conventional diagnostics while jointly determining the magnitude or significance of an estimated coefficient. MIS searches directly for the subset that produces the largest change in a specified regression quantity. This formulation addresses masking at the subset level, because observations are evaluated jointly rather than through a preliminary observation-level ranking. Subsequent work develops computational methods that exploit the structure of the linear-regression deletion problem \parencite{konrad2026finding}. Exact search gives the selected observations a precise interpretation: among all subsets of the same size, they generate the largest realised shift in the target coefficient.

For a prespecified subset size \(k\), the fixed-\(k\) MIS objective can be represented conceptually as
\[
S_k^*
\in
\arg\max_{\substack{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\ |S|=k}}
\left|
\widehat{\beta}_{j,-S}
-
\widehat{\beta}_{j}
\right|,
\]
where \(\widehat{\beta}_{j}\) is the full-sample estimate of the target coefficient and \(\widehat{\beta}_{j,-S}\) is its estimate after deleting \(S\). The absolute value admits movement in either direction. The objective is conditional on \(k\), so it identifies the most influential subset for a specified deletion budget. The deletion budget is treated as a sensitivity parameter and is not an estimate of a latent contamination count. Several values of \(k\) may be studied descriptively, but each optimisation remains a fixed-\(k\) problem. The algebraic reduction and exact Dinkelbach optimisation are developed in \autoref{sec:methodology}.

Exact optimisation identifies the maximising subset, while statistical inference addresses the magnitude of the resulting maximum. The reported influence is selected from \(\binom{n}{k}\) candidate subsets, so ordinary sampling variation can generate a large maximum. Selection changes the inferential reference point. Inference must therefore evaluate the fixed-\(k\) maximum under a null or reference model that reflects the search. Extreme Value Theory provides the framework used for that purpose.

\subsection{Fixed-\texorpdfstring{\(k\)}{k} maximum influence and statistical inference}
\label{subsec:fixed_k_inference}

Fixed-\(k\) MIS produces an extremal statistic because the reported subset is chosen from all admissible size-\(k\) deletions. Even when \(k\) is prespecified, the reported coefficient change is the maximum over many competing subsets. Its null behaviour therefore differs from that of a statistic attached to one prespecified deletion set. A large realised maximum can arise from ordinary sampling variation across a large candidate family. Statistical calibration must consequently target the distribution of the fixed-\(k\) maximum itself.

Extreme Value Theory provides a framework for analysing maxima and upper-tail events. Classical limit results show that suitably normalised block maxima converge to the generalised extreme-value family \parencite{fisher1928limiting,gnedenko1943distribution}. Maximum-likelihood methods provide one route for estimating the parameters of the GEV model \parencite{bucher2017maximum}. The candidate deletion sets overlap extensively, so the associated influence statistics are strongly dependent. The adequacy of an extreme-value approximation therefore depends on the specific MIS setting and requires calibration and empirical validation. Recent work adopts this approach for Most Influential Sets by treating maximum subset influence as the inferential object \parencite{konrad2026testing}. The resulting calibration and its numerical validation are developed in \autoref{sec:methodology}.

The deletion budget retains its sensitivity interpretation under extreme-value calibration. The test asks whether maximal influence is unusually large at the specified value of \(k\). Several deletion budgets may still be examined descriptively. Their maxima form an influence profile showing how coefficient sensitivity changes as the deletion budget increases. Each point on that profile remains a fixed-\(k\) quantity, so the analysis reports a sensitivity curve rather than an estimated subset size.

\subsection{Model misspecification and diagnostic sensitivity}
\label{subsec:model_misspecification}

Model misspecification matters because a regression specification is often an approximation to the population relationship. Under conditional-mean misspecification, least squares can converge to a well-defined linear approximation even when the fitted regression does not coincide with the population regression function \parencite{white1980using}. The resulting target coefficient remains interpretable within the fitted approximation, while its finite-sample sensitivity can depend on the mechanism and severity of the model failure. The distribution of that sensitivity across observations is therefore an empirical question. For coefficient-targeted set influence, the relevant finite-sample issue is how strongly a subset of prespecified size can move the target coefficient. Modern econometric work studies sensitivity to departures from a reference model and the consequences for estimation and inference \parencite{andrews2017measuring,bonhomme2022minimizing,armstrong2025adapting}. This literature motivates evaluating MIS across mechanisms that generate different patterns and severities of target-coefficient sensitivity.

Omitted variables and incorrect functional form alter the conditional-mean relationship represented by the fitted regression. An omitted variable can shift a target coefficient when the omitted component is associated with included regressors, while nonlinearities and missing interactions can make a linear coefficient summarise heterogeneous local relationships. Classical specification tests examine these departures through residual or conditional-moment restrictions. RESET targets broad specification errors through functions of fitted values \parencite{ramsey1969tests}. Consistent conditional-moment and nonparametric tests extend the analysis to general functional-form departures and omitted-variable structures \parencite{bierens1990consistent,fan1996consistent}. This literature clarifies the range of conditional-mean departures against which coefficient-targeted influence can be interpreted.

Coefficient heterogeneity creates misspecification when a common regression coefficient averages relationships that vary systematically across observations. Random-coefficient models formalise cross-sectional coefficient variation within a common regression framework \parencite{swamy1970efficient}. A related departure arises when the regression relationship changes across regions of the covariate space. Threshold regression represents this structure by allowing coefficients to change when an observed variable crosses an estimated threshold \parencite{hansen2000sample}. Heterogeneous slopes and threshold effects can therefore generate distinct patterns of target-coefficient sensitivity. Both mechanisms can associate coefficient sensitivity with identifiable subgroups or regions of the covariate space. They therefore provide natural settings for evaluating whether MIS localises the observations associated with the misspecified relationship.

Endogeneity and heteroskedasticity represent departures of a different kind for coefficient-targeted influence. Linear endogeneity creates dependence between the regressor and the disturbance without necessarily generating a small group of observations that carries the resulting coefficient distortion. Nonlinear confounding changes the form of this dependence and may generate a different distribution of coefficient sensitivity across observations. Heteroskedasticity changes the conditional variance while leaving a correctly specified conditional mean possible. The Breusch--Pagan test provides the mechanism-specific variance diagnostic used in the comparison \parencite{breusch1979simple}. These mechanisms therefore provide contrasting settings for evaluating how the form of the model departure affects the detection profile of MIS.

Modern sensitivity analysis treats robustness as dependent on the target parameter, the maintained assumptions, and the direction of model departure. \textcite{andrews2017measuring} measure how parameter estimates respond to perturbations in estimation moments, linking sensitivity to the identifying information used by the model. \textcite{bonhomme2022minimizing} study estimation and inference within neighbourhoods of a reference specification and make the magnitude of misspecification explicit in the analysis. \textcite{armstrong2025adapting} examine the trade-off between efficiency under stronger assumptions and robustness when those assumptions fail. These approaches share an implication for diagnostic comparison: different procedures target different consequences of misspecification, so their rejection profiles should be interpreted according to the statistical object each procedure measures.

The resulting research gap concerns the detection profile of coefficient-targeted set influence across qualitatively different model failures. MIS is studied as a coefficient-specific sensitivity diagnostic and is not proposed as a universal omnibus specification test. The empirical question is how the detection profile of MIS varies across misspecification mechanisms, severity levels, and deletion budgets, and where its response becomes weak or absent. Its comparators address different statistical objects. Cook's distance, DFBETAS, and leverage represent classical influence diagnostics, while RESET and the Breusch--Pagan test provide specification-focused benchmarks. Weak responses and non-detection are themselves informative because they identify settings in which coefficient-targeted set influence has limited detection power under the studied design. Detection also remains separate from correction, because identifying observations associated with misspecification does not establish that deleting them restores a correctly specified model.
