\subsection{Simulation DGPs}
\label{app:simulation-dgps}

The simulation studies use related distributional environments but differ in their substantive perturbations. This subsection records the principal data-generating-process definitions used in the simulation analyses.

For the robust-estimation and misspecification studies, sample size takes values
\[
n\in\{500,1000,2500,5000\}.
\]
The predictor distribution is one of three designs. The normal design draws \(X\sim N(0,1)\). The mixed-normal design draws \(90\%\) of observations from \(N(0,1)\) and \(10\%\) from \(N(0,10^2)\). The contaminated-normal design begins with \(N(0,1)\) draws and adds an independent \(N(0,50^2)\) perturbation to \(10\%\) of observations.

Eight error-distribution families are considered. Normal errors follow \(N(0,1)\). Mixed-normal errors combine \(90\%\) \(N(0,1)\) draws with \(10\%\) \(N(0,10^2)\) draws. Skewed-\(t\) errors use skewness parameter \(5\) and \(5\) degrees of freedom. The log-normal-mixture design combines \(70\%\) log-normal draws with log-scale parameters \((0,0.5)\) and \(30\%\) with parameters \((1,1.5)\). Beta errors follow Beta\((2,5)\). Generalised Pareto errors use location \(0\), scale \(1\), and shape \(0.25\) in the misspecification study. Contaminated-normal errors add an independent \(N(0,50^2)\) perturbation to \(10\%\) of otherwise standard-normal draws. Pareto errors use scale \(1\) and shape \(3\). Error draws are centred before construction of the regression outcome.

The contamination studies introduce three observation-level mechanisms. Response outliers shift the outcome while retaining ordinary predictor positions. Good-leverage observations occupy extreme predictor positions while remaining close to the fitted regression relation. Bad-leverage observations are displaced in both predictor and outcome space so that the planted observations jointly distort the target slope. The contamination proportion determines the planted-set size, with the exact simulation grids described in \autoref{subsec:detection-performance} and \autoref{subsec:estimation-performance}.

The misspecification study instead introduces departures from the fitted regression without planting a contamination set. The baseline fitted model targets the coefficient on \(x\). The eight mechanisms are defined as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \emph{Nonlinear functional form}: a centred quadratic component in \(x\) is added to the conditional mean while the fitted model remains linear.
	
	\item \emph{Threshold behaviour}: a hinge component \((x-c_0)_+\) is added above the \(75\)th percentile \(c_0\) of the predictor distribution.
	
	\item \emph{Omitted variable}: an auxiliary variable \(z\), correlated with \(x\) at approximately \(0.5\), enters the outcome but is omitted from the fitted regression.
	
	\item \emph{Heterogeneous slopes}: a randomly selected \(25\%\) subgroup receives an additional slope component through an \(x\)-by-group interaction.
	
	\item \emph{Missing interaction}: the fitted model includes \(x\) and \(z\) but omits an \(x z\) interaction that enters the data-generating process.
	
	\item \emph{Linear endogeneity}: the structural error is constructed to have a prespecified correlation with the standardised target regressor \(x\).
	
	\item \emph{Nonlinear confounding}: the structural error is related to the centred and standardised quadratic transformation of \(x\), breaking the fitted-span structure of the linear-endogeneity design.
	
	\item \emph{Heteroskedasticity}: the error scale increases with the absolute standardised value of \(x\), while the conditional mean remains correctly specified.
\end{enumerate}

For additive conditional-mean departures, severity scales the misspecification component relative to the dispersion of the baseline error. Linear and nonlinear endogeneity instead vary the dependence between the regressor transformation and the structural error, while the heteroskedastic design varies the conditional-error scale gradient. The exact severity grids used for the detection-boundary calculations are fixed independently of the MIS deletion fraction.

Both contamination studies use magnitude \(m=10\) and deletion size \(k=\max\{\lfloor cn\rfloor,2\}\). The recovery study uses \(c\in\{0.005,0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}\), while the robust-estimation study excludes \(c=0.10\). Perturbations use MAD-based scales with normal-consistency factor \(1.4826\) and lower bound \(10^{-4}\). Response outliers receive a common signed outcome shift of \(10s_y\). Good-leverage observations receive a common signed predictor shift of \(10s_x\), with outcomes placed near the clean-sample fitted line and Gaussian noise of standard deviation \(0.01s_y\). Bad-leverage observations receive the same predictor shift and a signed outcome displacement of \(10\max\{s_y,0.5|\beta_1x_i'|\}\) from the true regression relation, where \(x_i'\) denotes the shifted predictor.

The additive grid applies to nonlinear functional form, omitted variables, heterogeneous slopes, and missing interactions. Threshold behaviour and heteroskedasticity use their respective grids, while linear endogeneity and nonlinear confounding share the endogeneity grid. \label{app:numerical}

\subsection{Block-Maxima EVT Calibration}
\label{app:evt}

A block-maxima extreme-value construction \parencite{konrad2026testing} was evaluated as a candidate calibration device for fixed-\(k\) influence. For a tested set \(S\), the observations outside \(S\) are partitioned into \(B\) blocks, the fixed-\(k\) maximum of the set-DFBETA is computed within each block, a generalised extreme-value (GEV) distribution is fitted to the block maxima, and the p-value is the upper-tail probability of \(|\Delta(S)|\) under the fitted distribution. Three features of the evaluation explain why the construction is not used for inference in this thesis.

First, the tested set differs from the set that would be tested in practice. In the distributional simulation, the tested set is the injected set under contamination and a randomly drawn size-\(k\) set under the clean design. Neither is the data-selected MIS. The recorded rejection rates therefore describe the influence of a known or random set rather than the calibration of a selected maximum.

Second, the block construction degenerates over much of the simulation design. A block value is a maximum only when the block contains more than \(k\) observations. \autoref{tab:02A-block-count-summary} shows that the ratio of \(k\) to block size equals one in \(12\) of the \(20\) sample-size--contamination configurations, including every configuration with contamination of \(2.5\%\) or more. In these configurations each block admits a single size-\(k\) subset, so the fitted distribution describes whole-block deletion effects rather than maxima. \autoref{prop:block-ratio} gives the corresponding bound on the admissible deletion fraction. The aggregate empirical rejection rate of \(4.5\%\) recorded under the clean design averages over these degenerate configurations and is therefore not evidence of calibration.

\input{../../output/02_distributional/tables/supplement/02_tabA5_block_count_summary.tex}

Third, where the construction is well defined it is strongly conservative. In a dedicated null experiment across nine regression architectures, the empirical rejection rate at the nominal \(5\%\) level averaged \(0.2\%\) under constrained GEV fitting and \(0.6\%\) under robust fitting (\autoref{fig:03-null-calibration}), and a Kolmogorov--Smirnov test rejected uniformity of the null p-values at the \(1\%\) level in every design cell. GEV fitting converged in \(93.9\%\) and \(100\%\) of fits, respectively, so numerical convergence does not imply an accurate reference distribution.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{../../output/03_algorithmic/figures/main/03_fig3_null_calibration.pdf}
\caption{Empirical null rejection rates of the block-maxima EVT test across nominal significance levels for constrained and robust GEV fitting. The diagonal denotes equality between nominal and empirical rejection.}
\label{fig:03-null-calibration}
\end{figure}

The injected-set recovery results in \autoref{subsec:detection-performance} do not depend on the EVT step, and calibration in the misspecification study uses independently simulated null distributions instead. Because the thesis treats \(k\) as a sensitivity budget and reports deletion paths, it does not require a p-value for a selected set.

\subsection{Supplementary Robust-Estimation Results}
\label{app:estimation-results}

The robust-estimation simulation uses MIS as an oracle-assisted localisation benchmark. The deletion budget and direction are supplied from the data-generating process, while observation identities remain unknown to the search. The oracle audit confirms that deleting the injected set produces a much larger target-coefficient shift under bad leverage than under response-outlier or good-leverage contamination (\autoref{tab:robust-oracle-audit}). \subsection{Supplementary Misspecification Results}
\label{app:misspec-definitions}

For the additive misspecification mechanisms, severity is scaled so that the standard deviation of the imposed misspecification component relative to the baseline error standard deviation equals the severity parameter \(\lambda\). Heteroskedasticity, linear endogeneity, and nonlinear confounding use mechanism-specific severity parameters. Detection boundaries are therefore interpreted within the corresponding mechanism-specific scale.

Target-coefficient sensitivity is evaluated using
\[
T_k
=
\frac{
\left|
\widehat{\beta}_{-S_k}-\widehat{\beta}
\right|
}{
\widehat{\operatorname{se}}(\widehat{\beta})
},
\]
where the selected set is obtained from the fixed-residualised search and \(\widehat{\beta}_{-S_k}\) is evaluated by full-refit validation. The denominator is the standard error from the original fitted model and is held fixed as a reference scale. \(T_k\) is not treated as a Wald statistic because the deletion set is selected adaptively and the full-sample and post-deletion estimates are dependent.

Independent null simulations provide the environment-specific reference distribution. At the primary deletion fraction, empirical null rejection remains close to the nominal \(5\%\) level across the evaluated error distributions (\autoref{tab:05-size-by-error}).

\input{../../output/05_misspecification/tables/supplement/05_tabS01_size_by_error.tex}

The respecification check evaluates whether the MIS response persists after the corresponding model departure is incorporated into the fitted specification. Because the corrected models contain different regressors, each is evaluated against its own independently simulated null distribution.

\input{../../output/05_misspecification/tables/main/05_tab06_specificity.tex}

For an environment whose fitted power curve reaches \(0.80\), the detection boundary is the interpolated severity at which that level is first attained. Environments that do not reach the criterion within the simulated range are treated as right-censored. The complete sample-size pattern underlying \autoref{fig:05-boundary-by-n} is shown in \autoref{fig:05S-boundary-faceted}. The mechanism-specific differences reinforce the main-text conclusion that misspecification does not translate uniformly into target-coefficient sensitivity.
