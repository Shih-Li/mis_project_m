\section{Simulation Results}
\label{sec:simulation}

This section uses three simulation studies to examine complementary parts of the relationship between outlyingness and target-coefficient influence. The distributional-contamination study provides the core analysis by asking when conventional contamination outliers also become jointly influential for the target slope. A shorter robust-estimation study separates influential-set localisation from stable coefficient estimation. The final study examines how model misspecification can create concentrated target-coefficient influence without conventional injected contamination. Algorithmic and calibration checks support these analyses but do not constitute separate substantive findings.

\subsection{Outlyingness and Target-Coefficient Influence under Distributional Contamination}
\label{subsec:detection-performance}

The core simulation asks when conventional contamination outliers also become jointly influential for the target slope coefficient. Response outliers, good leverage, and bad leverage create different relationships between sample outlyingness and coefficient movement. Classical diagnostics describe residual extremeness, covariate leverage, or single-observation influence, while fixed-\(k\) MIS selects the size-\(k\) set that maximises target-coefficient change. Recovery of the injected observations therefore measures the alignment between contamination status and target-coefficient influence under each mechanism.

The figure contrasts visually unusual observations, single-observation diagnostics, and fixed-\(k\) MIS, illustrating a masking-like failure of casewise diagnostics. The coefficient comparison reports absolute target-slope error; contaminated OLS provides the undeleted baseline, while the deletion methods remove 20 observations.}
	\label{fig:classical-vs-mis-demo}

The design varies sample size \(n\in\{500,1000,2500,5000\}\) and contamination proportion \(c\in\{0.005,0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}\). The injected-set size is
\[
k=\max\{\lfloor cn\rfloor,2\}.
\]
The simulation therefore supplies oracle \(k\). For contaminated samples, the known injected set is also used to resolve the directional ambiguity of the fixed-\(k\) search. Observation identities are not supplied to the subsequent search itself. The resulting procedure is therefore an oracle-assisted localisation benchmark for both set size and direction. It is referred to below as MIS with oracle \(k\) and oracle direction.

The three contamination mechanisms differ in how they affect the target slope. Response outliers shift the outcome at ordinary covariate positions, while good-leverage observations move far into predictor space but remain approximately aligned with the regression relation. Bad leverage combines both features: high leverage is paired with a response shift that rotates the fitted slope. All methods are evaluated against the same injected set.

Bad leverage produced the clearest alignment between the MIS objective and the injected mechanism. In the equal-weight design-cell summary, MIS recovered \(92.9\%\) of the injected observations, compared with \(86.5\%\) for the highest-recovery classical diagnostic (\autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary}). Across the sample-size--contamination grid, the target-aware recovery difference remained positive and ranged from \(8.0\) to \(16.0\) percentage points (\autoref{fig:02-mis-advantage}). \autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary} always uses the highest classical recovery, while the figure uses the lowest classical benchmark for the two negative-control mechanisms. Bad leverage therefore provides the setting in which the injected set is most closely aligned with the coefficient-targeted MIS objective.

Response outliers produce the opposite recovery pattern. MIS recovered \(26.6\%\) of the injected observations, compared with \(48.4\%\) for the highest-recovery classical diagnostic (\autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary}). Here the classical procedures benefit from residual extremeness, while a large residual need not create strong target-slope influence. MIS therefore recovers fewer injected observations because its objective is tied to coefficient movement.

Good leverage produces a larger recovery gap. The highest-recovery classical diagnostic recovered \(70.1\%\) of the injected observations, while MIS recovered \(36.9\%\) (\autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary}). The injected observations are extreme in predictor space but remain approximately aligned with the fitted relation, so covariate extremeness need not translate into strong slope influence. This distinction motivates the separate mechanism diagnostic below.

A separate mechanism diagnostic examines this result at \(n=1000\), \(k=10\), with normal predictors and \(200\) Monte Carlo iterations per error distribution. MIS-selected observations typically had much lower leverage but substantially larger residual and absolute-DFBETA magnitudes than the injected good-leverage set (\autoref{fig:02-good-leverage-mechanism} and \autoref{tab:02-good-leverage-mechanism}). Under normal errors, for example, the median leverage ratio was \(0.07\), while the corresponding absolute-DFBETA and residual ratios were \(19.19\) and \(131.91\). The diagnostic therefore tracks coefficient influence more closely than covariate extremeness.

The distributional results preserve this mechanism-dependent interpretation while identifying conditions in which the separation weakens. Under bad leverage, MIS recovery exceeded \(97\%\) for normal, Beta\((2,5)\), mixed-normal, skewed-\(t\), and Pareto errors, but fell to \(78.5\%\) under GPD errors (\autoref{fig:02-distributional-robustness}). The simulated GPD shape parameters are \(\xi\in\{1.5,3,5,10\}\), for which the population mean does not exist. This condition is therefore treated separately as an extreme stress design. Auxiliary MIS-EVT calibration and convergence results are reported in \autoref{app:detection-evt-diagnostics}. The block-maxima construction deteriorates sharply as the deletion fraction increases and becomes fully degenerate at \(2.5\%\) and above in this design.

Overall, fixed-\(k\) MIS is most closely aligned with the injected set when the contamination mechanism creates concentrated joint influence on the target coefficient. Lower recovery under response-outlier and good-leverage contamination does not indicate failure of the coefficient-targeted diagnostic, because the injected set need not be the set that most strongly perturbs the target slope. The next simulation asks a separate question: whether successful localisation of coefficient-relevant observations translates into improved post-deletion coefficient estimation.











\subsection{Influential-Set Localisation and Robust Estimation}
\label{subsec:estimation-performance}

The second study separates influential-set localisation from coefficient estimation. It compares full-sample OLS, three classical diagnostic-removal procedures, fixed-\(k\) MIS, and two direct robust estimators. Leverage, Cook's distance, DFBETAS, and MIS select observations before an OLS refit, whereas MM regression and LTS modify estimation directly. The distinction matters because successful localisation and stable coefficient estimation need not move together.

The formal design varies sample size \(n\in\{500,1000,2500,5000\}\) and contamination proportion \(c\in\{0.005,0.01,0.025,0.05\}\), with three predictor environments and eight error distributions. The predictor designs are normal, a two-component normal mixture, and a contaminated-normal design; these differ from the predictor families used in the preceding distributional-recovery study. The full experiment contains \(1{,}248\) design cells with \(100\) Monte Carlo iterations per cell. Primary summaries exclude the infinite-mean GPD stress condition and give equal weight to the resulting finite-moment design cells.

The MIS benchmark conditions on two oracle quantities: the injected-set size and the direction of the target-coefficient perturbation induced by the injected set. Conditional on these quantities, the identities of the selected observations are determined by the Dinkelbach-based fixed-\(k\) search. The benchmark therefore isolates influential-set localisation from the separate problems of choosing \(k\) and choosing the search direction. Under no contamination, oracle \(k=0\), so MIS coincides with full-sample OLS by construction. The corresponding design and oracle checks are reported in \autoref{tab:robust-design-audit} and \autoref{tab:robust-oracle-audit}.

Direct robust estimators produced the smallest aggregate coefficient errors in the finite-moment comparison. Under bad leverage, full-sample OLS had mean absolute bias \(1.903\) and RMSE \(2.594\), while MIS with oracle \(k\) reduced these quantities to \(0.076\) and \(0.168\), respectively (\autoref{tab:robust-mean-absolute-bias} and \autoref{tab:robust-rmse}). Cook's distance and DFBETAS also improved substantially on OLS, but their corresponding mean absolute biases were \(0.296\) and \(0.242\). MM and LTS remained more accurate still, with bad-leverage mean absolute biases of \(0.007\) and \(0.008\). Mean coefficient estimates across all mechanisms are reported in \autoref{tab:robust-mean-coefficients}.

The pooled results conceal substantial predictor-distribution heterogeneity. Under bad leverage, OLS mean absolute bias ranged from \(3.533\) with normal predictors to \(0.374\) with contaminated-normal predictors. The relative ordering of the deletion procedures also changes across these environments: MIS compares favourably with Cook's distance and DFBETAS under normal and normal-mixture predictors, whereas the ordering reverses under the contaminated-normal predictor design (\autoref{tab:robust-bad-leverage-by-predictor} and \autoref{fig:04A-mis-oracle-vs-classical}). The contaminated-normal predictor design can generate extreme-leverage observations through the background predictor distribution itself, so the injected set is not necessarily the only source of target-coefficient sensitivity. Post-deletion accuracy therefore depends on the geometry of the observations that remain.

The coefficient-error distributions reinforce the distinction between aggregate accuracy and dispersion. MM and LTS remain tightly concentrated around zero across the primary finite-moment comparison, while MIS with oracle \(k\) has wider distributions under response-outlier and good-leverage contamination (\autoref{fig:04-coefficient-errors}). OLS shows the greatest deterioration under bad leverage. The condition-specific large-error diagnostic then identifies finite-moment predictor--error combinations in which MIS coefficient error exceeds \(0.5\) with non-negligible probability (\autoref{fig:04-mis-oracle-large-errors}). The infinite-mean GPD design is excluded from this primary figure and treated separately as a stress condition rather than as a regular finite-moment sampling regime.

The wide MIS distributions under response-outlier and good-leverage contamination do not conflict with the recovery results in the preceding study. The injected set can be unusual without materially distorting the slope. In the present study, MIS is nevertheless required to select and delete exactly \(k\) observations. When little coefficient distortion needs repair, selecting the size-\(k\) set that maximises coefficient movement can perturb an otherwise stable estimate. Bad leverage differs because the injected set itself generates substantial slope distortion, bringing localisation and post-deletion estimation into closer alignment.

Coverage requires separate interpretation for direct estimators and deletion-based procedures. Under bad leverage, MM retained \(95.0\%\) coverage, MIS with oracle \(k\) recorded \(78.1\%\), and full-sample OLS fell to \(9.6\%\) (\autoref{tab:robust-coverage}). The remaining diagnostic-deletion procedures showed similarly intermediate coverage. Because these intervals are ordinary OLS intervals computed after data-dependent deletion, their reported coverage describes interval behaviour after selection without adjustment for that selection step. Good leverage shows a different pattern: MM and LTS retained small point-estimation error while their coverage fell to \(69.5\%\) and \(65.5\%\). Point accuracy and interval calibration can therefore move separately.

\input{../../output/04_robust/tables/main/04_tab2c_coverage.tex}

Influential-set localisation reveals the diagnostic information provided by fixed-\(k\) MIS. Under bad leverage, MIS recovered \(96.0\%\) of the injected set while selecting exactly the oracle set size (\autoref{tab:robust-detection-efficiency}). Cook's distance and DFBETAS achieved slightly higher recall, at \(98.2\%\) and \(98.4\%\), but their mean selection-size ratios were \(3.19\) and \(3.32\). Their corresponding precisions were \(43.9\%\) and \(42.3\%\), compared with \(96.0\%\) for MIS. Leverage selected almost five times the injected-set size while recovering \(67.7\%\) of it, and the full recall and precision distributions appear in \autoref{fig:04-detection-quality}.

Because MIS selects exactly \(k\) observations when the injected set also has size \(k\), its precision and recall coincide; both represent injected-set recovery. Recovery is much lower under response-outlier and good-leverage contamination, consistent with the narrower coefficient-targeted objective. The oracle audit helps explain this contrast: the mean absolute coefficient shift produced by deleting the true injected set is \(1.902\) under bad leverage, compared with \(0.020\) for response outliers and \(0.003\) for good leverage.

The second study therefore separates three statistical objectives. MM and LTS provide the strongest aggregate point-estimation performance, classical deletion diagnostics can obtain high recall by selecting substantially larger sets, and fixed-\(k\) MIS provides a more selective coefficient-targeted localisation benchmark under bad leverage. High injected-set recovery need not produce the smallest post-deletion coefficient error because the refitted coefficient also depends on the geometry of the remaining sample. Both studies so far introduce contamination into an otherwise correctly specified regression model; the final study asks how coefficient sensitivity behaves when the specification itself is wrong.


\subsection{Misspecification-Induced Target-Coefficient Influence}
\label{subsec:misspecification}

The third simulation asks whether model misspecification can generate concentrated target-coefficient influence without conventional injected contamination outliers. Eight mechanisms are considered: linear endogeneity, nonlinear confounding, heterogeneous slopes, heteroskedasticity, a missing interaction, nonlinear functional form, an omitted variable, and threshold behaviour. Each mechanism is evaluated across \(96\) environments formed by four sample sizes, three predictor distributions, and eight error distributions. These designs extend the preceding contamination study by allowing coefficient sensitivity to arise from the fitted specification itself.

Fixed-\(k\) MIS is evaluated at deletion fractions \(k/n\in\{0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}\), with \(k/n=0.025\) used as the primary operating point. The MIS search selects \(S_k\) under the fixed-residualised objective described in \autoref{sec:methodology}; the selected set is then evaluated by refitting the full simulated model. Target-coefficient sensitivity is summarised by
\[
T_k
=
\frac{
	\left|
	\widehat{\beta}_{-S_k}-\widehat{\beta}
	\right|
}{
	\widehat{\operatorname{se}}(\widehat{\beta})
},
\]
where \(\widehat{\beta}_{-S_k}\) is the full-refit target coefficient after deleting the selected set. Independent null simulations provide the reference distribution for \(T_k\). The remaining deletion fractions are treated as alternative sensitivity budgets rather than estimates of an underlying influential-set size.

The response of fixed-\(k\) MIS is strongly mechanism dependent. At the primary deletion fraction, nonlinear confounding, heterogeneous slopes, nonlinear functional form, and threshold behaviour reached the \(80\%\) detection criterion in all \(96\) environments, while the omitted-variable design reached it in only \(8.3\%\) of environments. Linear endogeneity never reached the criterion. The severity required to attain this response also differs across mechanisms, indicating that misspecification produces concentrated target-coefficient influence only under particular forms of departure.

Figure~\ref{fig:05-boundary-by-n} shows how the severity required to attain the \(80\%\) criterion varies with sample size. For nonlinear confounding, heterogeneous slopes, missing interactions, nonlinear functional form, and threshold behaviour, the required severity generally falls as sample size increases. Heteroskedasticity shows the same pattern beyond the smallest design. Omitted-variable misspecification remains substantially weaker, while linear endogeneity does not attain the criterion within the simulated severity range.

The deletion budget \(k\) remains a sensitivity parameter. Changing \(k/n\) changes the set over which influence is optimised and therefore changes the operating characteristics of the diagnostic. The simulated deletion fractions are interpreted as alternative fixed-budget sensitivity analyses; their relative performance does not identify a uniquely correct influential-set size.

Linear endogeneity provides a boundary case for this interpretation. In the simulated design, the endogeneity component lies within the fitted linear span, so increasing its severity changes the coefficient representation without changing the relevant deletion-sensitivity structure. The MIS statistic is consequently invariant up to numerical precision across severity levels. Nonlinear confounding removes this fitted-span invariance and produces a strong response across the recorded environments. Misspecification alone is therefore insufficient to generate concentrated target-coefficient influence; the form of the departure determines whether sensitivity becomes concentrated in particular observations. The boundary analysis establishes whether misspecification generates a concentrated target-coefficient sensitivity signal. A separate question is whether the observations selected by MIS coincide with a substantively defined affected subgroup. This comparison is possible only when the simulation defines such a subgroup directly.

Localisation can be evaluated directly only when the simulation defines an observation-level affected subgroup. The threshold design provides the clearest case: at \(k/n=0.025\), the MIS-selected set has precision \(0.606\) and lift \(2.43\) relative to an affected fraction of \(0.25\). Heterogeneous slopes produce a different pattern, with precision \(0.274\) and lift \(1.10\), close to the no-enrichment benchmark. A mechanism can therefore generate substantial target-coefficient sensitivity without causing the selected MIS observations to coincide closely with a nominal affected subgroup. Detection of coefficient sensitivity and localisation of a known subgroup are distinct properties.

Localisation of target-coefficient influence does not provide a correction for model misspecification. The lower panels of \autoref{fig:05-targeting-localisation} show that coefficient RMSE can increase as the deletion budget grows, even after diagnostically selected observations are removed and the model is refitted. The relevant comparisons are within predictor environments because the panels use different vertical scales. Deletion changes the estimating sample, whereas correction of misspecification requires changes to the maintained specification or identifying assumptions. The localisation results therefore describe where finite-sample coefficient sensitivity is concentrated rather than prescribing removal of the selected observations.

The misspecification study extends the distinction established by the contamination designs. \autoref{fig:05-boundary-by-n} shows that model misspecification can generate concentrated target-coefficient sensitivity without conventional injected contamination outliers, but that this response depends strongly on the form of the departure. \autoref{fig:05-targeting-localisation} shows a second distinction: strong coefficient sensitivity need not imply close correspondence between the selected influential set and a nominal affected subgroup, and deleting selected observations does not in general repair the misspecified model. Fixed-\(k\) MIS therefore characterises one consequence of misspecification, namely its expression through finite-sample joint influence on a prespecified coefficient.
