\section{Simulation Results}
\label{sec:simulation}

This section evaluates the MIS framework using two complementary simulation exercises. The first exercise evaluates whether MIS and classical diagnostics recover observations deliberately injected into the sample. The second exercise evaluates whether deleting the observations selected by each method and refitting the model improves recovery of the true slope coefficient. Although related, the two exercises assess different outcomes: a method may successfully flag unusual observations yet produce an inaccurate post-deletion estimator. Throughout the section, the tables and figures use the shorter simulation labels ``vertical outliers'', ``good leverage'', and ``bad leverage''. The text interprets these labels as response outliers, on-model high-leverage observations, and slope-distorting high-leverage observations, respectively. The revised terminology emphasises the substantive role of each scenario while leaving the underlying simulation design unchanged.

The simulation design uses controlled contamination scenarios to distinguish among different definitions of an outlier. Response outliers shift the outcome while leaving predictor values unchanged, producing unusually large residuals at ordinary covariate positions. On-model high-leverage observations lie far in predictor space but remain approximately aligned with the fitted regression relation, making them unusual yet internally consistent. Slope-distorting high-leverage observations lie far in predictor space and shift the outcome in a direction that can rotate the fitted slope. MIS targets the subset with the largest effect on the coefficient of interest, while classical diagnostics respond more directly to residual size, leverage, or single-observation coefficient changes. The detection results therefore compare coefficient-targeted outlier detection with broader residual- and leverage-based screening.

\subsection{Outlier Detection}
\label{subsec:detection-performance}

MIS can be used as an outlier detection tool when an outlier is defined by its effect on the target coefficient. Cook's distance measures changes in the fitted model after single-observation deletion; leverage identifies unusual predictor values; and DFBETAS measures coefficient sensitivity to individual observations. MIS identifies the set whose removal produces the largest change in the coefficient of interest. The comparison therefore evaluates recovery of injected observations that threaten the slope, while recognising that classical diagnostics target broader forms of unusualness.

The detection design varies sample size \(n \in \{500,1000,2500,5000\}\) and contamination proportion \(c \in \{0.005,0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}\); the injected set size is \(k=\max\{\lfloor cn\rfloor,2\}\). All methods are evaluated against the same injected set in each design cell.

MIS performed best when the injected observations jointly distorted the coefficient of interest. Under slope-distorting high-leverage contamination, labelled as bad leverage in the tables and figures, MIS recovered an average of \(92.9\%\) of the injected observations, versus \(76.0\%\) for the strongest classical diagnostic, a \(16.9\)-percentage-point advantage (\autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary}). This advantage remained positive throughout the grid of sample sizes and contamination proportions, ranging from approximately \(8.0\) to \(14.9\) percentage points in \autoref{fig:02-mis-advantage}. MIS therefore achieved its clearest detection gain when influence on the target coefficient was most relevant.

For response outliers and on-model high-leverage observations, the strongest classical diagnostic performed better. MIS recovered \(26.6\%\) of response outliers and \(36.9\%\) of on-model high-leverage observations, falling \(21.2\) and \(33.2\) percentage points below the best classical diagnostic, respectively. The heatmap confirms that this weaker recovery was systematic: the MIS advantage ranged from \(-36.0\) to \(-12.8\) percentage points for vertical outliers and from \(-45.2\) to \(-21.9\) percentage points for good leverage. These lower recovery rates follow from the MIS objective. Large response shocks and high-leverage observations aligned with the empirical regression relation can be outlying in residual or covariate terms while exerting little influence on the target coefficient. MIS should therefore be interpreted as a coefficient-targeted detector with a narrower purpose than general outlier screening. MIS is most effective for observations that jointly alter the coefficient of interest, as under slope-distorting high-leverage contamination.

The near-uniform EVT convergence rate of \(99.7\%\) across contaminated scenarios makes numerical fitting failures an unlikely explanation for the observed selection pattern. The pattern is consistent with substantive differences between coefficient-targeted MIS selection and the broader notions of outlyingness measured by classical diagnostics. Complete distribution-specific comparisons and the diagnostic analysis of the good-leverage mechanism are reported in \autoref{app:simulation}.









\subsection{Coefficient Estimation after Diagnostic Removal}% 04
\label{subsec:estimation-performance}

In the estimation exercise, most detection methods serve as diagnostic-removal procedures. First, OLS is fitted to the contaminated sample. Cook's distance, leverage, DFBETAS, and the MIS-based procedures then select observations for removal, and OLS is refitted on the retained sample. The resulting coefficient is a post-diagnostic OLS estimate. The MM-estimator and LTS use a different construction: they modify the estimating criterion directly, while the diagnostic-removal procedures delete a selected set and refit OLS. The MIS-based estimates should consequently be interpreted as hybrid deletion estimators: their performance depends not only on the MIS objective, but also on the rule used to choose the set size, the direction of coefficient movement, the stopping rule, and the final OLS refit.

The MIS procedures differ mainly in how they determine the deletion set size. Adaptive \(k\) estimates the set size from robust residuals before applying MIS. Iterative peeling removes observations in small batches and refits the model until a stopping rule is reached. MIS-SAP searches across candidate values of \(k\) and accepts a set only if the global selection-adjusted permutation test rejects. Oracle \(k\) fixes the set size at the known number of injected observations and serves only as a simulation benchmark. Accordingly, the comparison assesses alternative rules for selecting the MIS deletion set under a common definition of influence.

The estimation comparison is therefore best read as a fixed stress test of post-diagnostic removal rules. In this exercise, the sample size is held at \(n=5000\), the injected set size is fixed at \(k=50\), and the contamination magnitude is fixed at \(10\). These settings create a demanding environment in which the deletion rule can materially affect the refitted slope. The comparison does not cover all possible MIS-based estimators. Alternative deletion directions, stopping rules, and refitting targets could produce different finite-sample behaviour. The current MIS-SAP specification is a single-step SAP-gated deletion rule because it permits one accepted SAP removal step before the final OLS refit. The results below therefore characterise the implemented deletion estimators; they do not establish an intrinsic point-estimation property of MIS.

Among the considered simulation designs, LTS produced the most accurate and stable point estimates. Its mean absolute bias ranged from \(0.004\) to \(0.009\) across the four contamination mechanisms, with RMSE between \(0.008\) and \(0.020\). The MM-estimator was the next most stable procedure, with mean absolute bias between \(0.009\) and \(0.020\) and RMSE between \(0.137\) and \(0.342\), substantially higher than the RMSE of LTS (\autoref{tab:robust-mean-absolute-bias} and~\ref{tab:robust-rmse}). \autoref{fig:04-coefficient-errors} visualises the same pattern: the coefficient errors of LTS and the MM-estimator remained concentrated near zero under each contamination mechanism. Thus, direct robust estimation provided the most reliable point estimates in this stress-test comparison.

The diagnostic-removal estimators were less stable because their performance depended on the selected deletion set. The MIS-based estimators, OLS, and classical-diagnostic deletion estimators exhibited substantially longer error tails in \autoref{fig:04-coefficient-errors}. For several MIS procedures, the medians remained close to zero while the arithmetic means were displaced by a small number of extremely large estimates. This pattern indicates that rare tail failures primarily drove the pooled errors, with little evidence of persistent central bias. The key distinction lies between central performance, where many deletion estimators remained close to the true coefficient, and tail reliability, where direct robust estimators were more stable.

No rule for selecting \(k\) dominated across all outcomes among the MIS variants. Under bad leverage, adaptive \(k\) generally removed the fewest observations, whereas iterative peeling recovered more of the injected set and achieved better coverage than the other data-adaptive MIS procedures. MIS-SAP selected larger sets without increasing overlap. Oracle \(k\) achieved the highest recovery; however, its coefficient estimates still exhibited severe tail failures. Thus, knowing the correct set size did not ensure selection of the correct observations or reliable coefficient recovery. Complete comparisons of coefficient accuracy, selected set size, overlap, stopping behaviour, and runtime are reported in \autoref{app:estimation-comparisons}, \autoref{app:estimation-selection}, \autoref{app:mis-sap-diagnostics}, and \autoref{app:estimation-computation}.

Overall, direct robust estimators were the most reliable point-estimation procedures in the stress-test design, whereas post-diagnostic deletion estimators were more vulnerable to rare but severe tail failures. The next subsection examines where those tail failures occurred and whether they reflected statistical instability or numerical non-completion.

\subsection{Failure Modes and Numerical Availability}
\label{subsec:failure-modes}

MIS-SAP's large errors clustered in a small number of identifiable data-generating conditions. Outside the GPD designs and contaminated-predictor scenarios with slope-distorting high-leverage contamination, labelled as bad leverage in the tables and figures, the probability that \(\lvert\widehat{\beta}-\beta_0\rvert>0.5\) was generally zero and never exceeded \(10\%\). In contrast, this probability reached \(100\%\) under GPD errors for every predictor distribution and contamination mechanism. Under slope-distorting high-leverage contamination with contaminated predictors, it ranged from \(50\%\) to \(59\%\) across the non-GPD error distributions (\autoref{fig:04-mis-sap-large-errors}). \autoref{fig:04-mis-sap-large-errors} therefore shows that the long tails in \autoref{fig:04-coefficient-errors} were concentrated in extreme-tail errors and contaminated-predictor leverage designs.

These failure modes arose from statistical instability while the numerical procedure completed successfully. The recorded MIS-SAP error rate of \(0.0\%\) across all contamination mechanisms confirms procedural completion; coefficient accuracy must be assessed using the bias and coverage results. Under slope-distorting high-leverage contamination and non-GPD errors, the mean absolute bias ranged from \(0.183\) to \(0.341\), while empirical coverage fell to between \(58.3\%\) and \(62.3\%\). Under GPD errors, the mean absolute bias became extremely large across all contamination mechanisms. The deterioration reflects sensitivity to extreme tails and structurally influential contamination, while the numerical implementation remained available. Complete MIS-SAP selection, stopping, and computational diagnostics are reported in \autoref{app:simulation}.
