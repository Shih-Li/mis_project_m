\section{Simulation Evidence}
\label{sec:simulation}

The simulations examine how target-coefficient sensitivity arises, how influence localisation relates to estimation, and how model misspecification can generate similar sensitivity. The three studies address these questions sequentially. The first evaluates when conventional contamination outliers coincide with observations carrying substantial joint influence on the target slope. The second examines how localisation of those observations relates to post-deletion coefficient estimation and robust estimation. The third studies whether model misspecification can generate concentrated coefficient sensitivity without a planted contamination set. Algorithmic and calibration checks support these analyses and are reported separately where appropriate.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Outlyingness and Target-Coefficient Influence}
\label{subsec:detection-performance}

The first simulation examines how outlyingness aligns with target-coefficient influence under different contamination mechanisms. Response outliers, good leverage, and bad leverage generate distinct relationships between unusualness and target-slope movement. Classical diagnostics rank observations using residual extremeness, leverage, or single-observation influence, while fixed-\(k\) MIS searches jointly for the size-\(k\) set that maximises target-coefficient movement. For a selected set \(S\) and injected set \(S_{\mathrm{inj}}\), each of size \(k\), injected-set overlap is defined as
\[
\operatorname{Overlap}(S,S_{\mathrm{inj}})
=
\frac{|S\cap S_{\mathrm{inj}}|}{k}.
\]
This quantity is the proportion of the injected set recovered by \(S\). Its interpretation is mechanism specific. Under bad leverage, the injected set itself carries substantial joint target-coefficient influence, so higher overlap indicates closer localisation of the influential contamination set. Under response outliers and good leverage, deleting the injected set produces comparatively limited target-slope movement. Higher overlap in these designs therefore indicates greater agreement with the planted contamination set, although those observations need not form the most influential set for the target coefficient.

The figure contrasts visually unusual observations, single-observation diagnostics, and fixed-\(k\) MIS, illustrating a masking-like failure of casewise diagnostics. The coefficient comparison reports absolute target-slope error; contaminated OLS provides the undeleted baseline, while the deletion methods remove 20 observations.}

The design varies sample size \(n\in\{500,1000,2500,5000\}\) and contamination proportion \(c\in\{0.005,0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}\). The injected-set size is
\[
k=\max\{\lfloor cn\rfloor,2\}.
\]
The simulation supplies the true injected-set size \(k\). For contaminated samples, the search runs in both directions, and the solution with greater overlap with the known injected set is retained. The identities of the contaminated observations are not supplied to the search itself. Under response outliers and good leverage, this selection rule favours injected-set recovery. The reported recovery is therefore an upper bound within the two searched directions, making the restraint comparisons conservative. The resulting procedure is therefore an oracle-assisted localisation benchmark for both set size and direction. It is referred to below as MIS with oracle \(k\) and oracle direction.

The linear-fractional representation in \autoref{eq:linear_fractional_objective} helps explain why MIS responds differently across the three contamination mechanisms. Each observation enters the directional deletion objective through two distinct components: its score contribution $w_i = \tilde{x}_i \tilde{r}_i$ and its design contribution $c_i = \tilde{x}_i^2$. For the positive direction, the Dinkelbach criterion ranks observations using
\begin{equation}
	s_i(\eta) = w_i + \eta c_i,
	\label{eq:dinkelbach_score}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is the current Dinkelbach parameter. At convergence, it equals the optimised directional influence. For the negative direction, $w_i$ is replaced by $-w_i$. The three mechanisms load onto these two terms in fundamentally different ways:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Bad leverage:} Planted observations are displaced simultaneously in covariate space and in response space. Consequently, each contaminated point carries both a large design weight $c_i$ and a large score contribution $w_i$ of the same sign. By \autoref{lem:superadditive}, joint deletion amplifies same-signed contributions relative to separate single-observation deletions. The injected set is therefore a strong candidate for the fixed-\(k\) maximiser.
	\item \textbf{Response outliers:} Contaminated observations receive a uniform outcome shift but remain at ordinary covariate positions. Their residuals are large, but the values of $\widetilde{x}_i$ have both positive and negative signs. Consequently, their score contributions $w_i = \tilde{x}_i \tilde{r}_i$ split between opposing directions and largely cancel when the full planted set is removed. A directional search can capture only the subset of planted points sharing a common sign, naturally bounding overlap well below 100\%.
	\item \textbf{Good leverage:} Observations occupy extreme positions in covariate space ($c_i$ is large) but lie directly along the regression line ($\tilde{r}_i \approx 0$, so $w_i \approx 0$). They can enter the selected set through the design term $\eta c_i$, which amplifies the influence of other deleted observations by reducing the residualised design information in the denominator. The injected good-leverage set therefore produces little direct target-slope movement. However, its design contribution can affect which deletions become influential as the deletion budget increases.
\end{itemize}
High injected-set recovery is therefore most consistently expected under bad leverage in these designs.

To account for these differing mechanics, the classical comparator is chosen to match the specific dimension targeted by each mechanism: Cook's distance for response outliers, leverage for good leverage, and the cell-wise best-performing classical diagnostic (the upper-envelope classical benchmark) for bad leverage. These comparisons characterise alignment between diagnostic objectives and target-coefficient influence, rather than a common performance ranking across contamination mechanisms.

Under bad leverage, planted points distort the target slope substantially. In the equal-weight design-cell summary, MIS recovers \(92.9\%\) of the injected observations, compared with \(86.5\%\) for the oracle classical benchmark (\autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary}). The detection margin over this benchmark was positive in every sample-size--contamination configuration, ranging from \(3.7\) to \(8.3\) percentage points and narrowing as sample size and contamination increased (\autoref{fig:02-mis-advantage}). The benchmark diagnostic itself changes across the grid: \dfbetas{} performs best at contamination proportions up to \(2.5\%\), and leverage at \(5\%\) and \(10\%\), where the injected coalition is large and extreme enough in predictor space to be flagged individually. Against any single classical diagnostic, the margin is substantially larger: \(16.9\) percentage points over \dfbetas{}, \(22.9\) over leverage, and \(23.8\) over Cook's distance. MIS therefore recovers more of the injected coalition than an oracle choice among the classical diagnostics, and a considerably larger share than any one of them applied uniformly. The bad-leverage design therefore shows close correspondence between the planted contamination mechanism and the set carrying the greatest target-slope influence.

Response outliers produce substantially weaker alignment between contamination status and target-slope influence. The MIS set overlaps \(26.6\%\) of the injected observations, compared with \(47.8\%\) for Cook's distance (\autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary}). Cook's distance flags these observations through their residual extremeness, but their joint effect on the slope is small. In the estimation study, which uses the same injection mechanism and magnitude, deleting the full injected set moved the target slope by \(0.020\) on average, compared with \(1.902\) under bad leverage (\autoref{tab:robust-oracle-audit}). Because the injected set is one of the candidate sets, the fixed-\(k\) maximiser in \autoref{eq:absolute_fixed_k_influence} moves the target coefficient at least as much as the injected set does under the fixed-residualised objective. Across the sample-size--contamination grid, the recovery gap between Cook's distance and MIS ranged from \(10\) to \(34\) percentage points and widened with the contamination proportion and, more mildly, with sample size (\autoref{fig:02-mis-advantage}). The widening reflects Cook's distance becoming more attracted to the injected observations as they become more numerous, while MIS recovery remains bounded by the directional split described above. Even \dfbetas{}, which targets the slope, recovered \(36.8\%\). This is consistent with single-case ranking selecting injected observations on both sides of the predictor mean, whereas joint deletion recognises that their contributions cancel. Low recovery in this design is the expected behaviour of a coefficient-targeted objective: the injected observations are unusual but do not form the most influential size-\(k\) set.

Good leverage produces the starkest divergence between sample extremeness and target influence. Leverage overlaps \(70.1\%\) of the injected observations, while the MIS set overlaps \(36.9\%\) (\autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary}). The injected observations occupy extreme predictor positions but lie close to the fitted relation, so deleting the full injected set moved the target slope by only \(0.003\) on average in the oracle audit (\autoref{tab:robust-oracle-audit}). Leverage-based rankings recover them because they are extreme in the predictor space, not because they carry coefficient influence. The recovery gap between leverage and MIS ranged from \(22\) to \(45\) percentage points and narrowed as the contamination proportion increased (\autoref{fig:02-mis-advantage}). This narrowing is consistent with the design channel: at larger deletion budgets, removing the stabilising high-leverage observations becomes part of the most influential deletion. The mechanism diagnostic below examines the separation directly.

A separate mechanism diagnostic examines this result at \(n=1000\), \(k=10\), with normal predictors and \(200\) Monte Carlo iterations per error distribution. MIS-selected observations typically had much lower leverage but substantially larger residual and absolute-DFBETA magnitudes than the injected good-leverage set (\autoref{fig:02-good-leverage-mechanism}); the distribution-specific summaries are reported in \autoref{tab:02-good-leverage-mechanism}. Under normal errors, for example, the median leverage ratio was \(0.07\), while the corresponding absolute-DFBETA and residual ratios were \(19.19\) and \(131.91\). The diagnostic therefore tracks coefficient influence more closely than covariate extremeness. Recovery of the good-leverage set nevertheless rises broadly with the tail weight of the error distribution, from \(0\%\) under normal errors to \(90\%\) under generalised Pareto distribution (GPD) errors, and the leverage ratio rises with it (\autoref{tab:02-good-leverage-mechanism}). This pattern is consistent with the design channel described above: heavier-tailed errors produce larger attainable coefficient movements \(\eta\), which increases the weight \(\eta c_i\) on high-leverage observations in the Dinkelbach ranking.

The remaining panels compare absolute DFBETA, leverage, and residual magnitude between the MIS-selected and injected sets on a base-two logarithmic ratio scale; zero denotes equality.}

The mechanism-dependent recovery pattern remains stable across most of the recorded error distributions. Under bad leverage, MIS recovery exceeded \(97\%\) for normal, Beta\((2,5)\), mixed-normal, skewed-\(t\), and Pareto errors, was \(91.6\%\) under log-normal-mixture errors and \(84.4\%\) under contaminated errors, and fell to \(78.5\%\) under GPD errors (\autoref{fig:02-distributional-robustness}). MIS recovery exceeded that of the classical benchmark under every error distribution. The simulated GPD shape parameters are \(\xi\in\{1.5,3,5,10\}\), for which the population mean does not exist. The design uses one shape grid for all parametric error families; for the skewed-\(t\) and Pareto errors larger values imply lighter tails, whereas for the GPD they imply heavier tails, so \(\xi=10\) is the most extreme case in the design rather than the closest to normality. This condition therefore serves as an extreme stress design. The error-distribution breakdown is reported in \autoref{app:detection-results}.


Taken together, these results show why injected-set recovery must be interpreted in light of the contamination mechanism. The next simulation examines what influence localisation implies for subsequent coefficient estimation.

\subsection{Influence Localisation and Estimation}
\label{subsec:estimation-performance}

The second simulation examines how influence localisation relates to coefficient estimation under contamination. Leverage, Cook's distance, \dfbetas{}, and fixed-\(k\) MIS select observations before an OLS refit. MM regression and LTS estimate the coefficient using resistant fitting rules. The comparison therefore separates two statistical objectives: locating observations that carry coefficient influence and stabilising coefficient estimation under contamination.

The design varies sample size \(n\in\{500,1000,2500,5000\}\), contamination proportion \(c\in\{0.005,0.01,0.025,0.05\}\), three predictor environments, and eight error distributions. The primary comparison uses the finite-moment design cells and treats the infinite-mean GPD condition separately as a stress case. MIS is evaluated as an oracle-assisted localisation benchmark. The search is supplied with the injected-set size \(k\) and the direction of the induced target-coefficient perturbation, but not with the identities of the injected observations. This isolates localisation conditional on the deletion budget and search direction. Under no contamination, oracle \(k=0\), so MIS coincides with full-sample OLS by construction. The oracle \(k=0\) rule applies only to MIS. Leverage, Cook's distance, and \dfbetas{} retain their prespecified threshold-based deletion rules. They can therefore remove observations even in an uncontaminated sample. MM regression and LTS instead use resistant fitting criteria without an oracle deletion budget.

\caption{Empirical distributions of signed target-coefficient error across methods and contamination mechanisms in the primary finite-moment designs. Separation between the two summaries can indicate asymmetry or the influence of infrequent large coefficient errors on average performance.}
\label{fig:04-coefficient-errors}
\end{figure}

The median describes the centre of each simulated error distribution, while the arithmetic mean responds more strongly to asymmetry and infrequent large errors. Separation between the two markers therefore highlights differences between typical and average coefficient error, providing context for the comparisons across contamination mechanisms below.

The estimation consequences of localisation depend on whether the injected observations themselves drive target-coefficient distortion. Under response-outlier and good-leverage contamination, the injected observations can be unusual while exerting limited influence on the target slope. Conditional on oracle \(k\) and direction, MIS selects the size-\(k\) set that maximises the fixed-residualised coefficient movement in that direction. The subsequent OLS refit can therefore move a coefficient that was already close to its target value. Bad leverage produces a different relationship because the injected set itself generates substantial target-slope distortion. Localisation and post-deletion coefficient accuracy are therefore most closely aligned when the contamination mechanism also drives coefficient error.

Under response-outlier contamination, post-MIS OLS has mean absolute error \(0.203\) and RMSE \(0.416\), compared with \(0.057\) and \(0.150\) for full-sample OLS. Under good leverage, post-MIS OLS has mean absolute error \(0.096\) and RMSE \(0.221\), compared with \(0.049\) and \(0.152\) for full-sample OLS. Bad leverage reverses this pattern: deleting the MIS-selected set reduces mean absolute error from \(1.903\) to \(0.076\) and RMSE from \(2.594\) to \(0.168\). Direct robust estimators remain more stable in this design; under bad leverage, MM and LTS produce mean absolute errors of \(0.007\)--\(0.008\) and RMSEs of \(0.015\)--\(0.016\).

Even when MIS deletion reduces coefficient error, as under bad leverage, the resulting OLS refit can remain less accurate than MM or LTS. Having examined outlyingness, influence localisation, and estimation under planted contamination, the final simulation turns to model misspecification as another source of concentrated coefficient sensitivity.

\subsection{Misspecification and Concentrated Coefficient Sensitivity}
\label{subsec:misspecification}

The third simulation examines whether model misspecification can generate concentrated coefficient sensitivity without a planted contamination set. Eight mechanisms are considered: linear endogeneity, nonlinear confounding, heterogeneous slopes, heteroskedasticity, a missing interaction, nonlinear functional form, an omitted variable, and threshold behaviour. Each mechanism is evaluated across \(96\) environments formed by four sample sizes \(n\in\{500,1000,2500,5000\}\), three predictor distributions---normal, mixed normal, and contaminated normal---and eight error distributions---normal, mixed normal, skewed-\(t\), log-normal mixture, Beta\((2,5)\), generalised Pareto, contaminated normal, and Pareto. The full distributional parameterisations and mechanism-specific data-generating processes are documented in \autoref{app:simulation-dgps}.

Fixed-\(k\) MIS is evaluated at deletion fractions \(k/n\in\{0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}\), with \(k/n=0.025\) used as the primary operating point. The MIS search produces one size-\(k\) candidate set in each coefficient direction under the fixed-residualised objective described in \autoref{sec:methodology}. Both candidates are then evaluated by full-refit validation, and \(S_k\) denotes the candidate producing the larger absolute movement in the refitted target coefficient. Target-coefficient sensitivity is summarised by
\[
T_k
=
\frac{
\left|
\widehat{\beta}_{-S_k}-\widehat{\beta}
\right|
}{
\widehat{\operatorname{se}}(\widehat{\beta})
},
\]
The statistic \(T_k\) measures full-refit coefficient movement in standard-error units. Here, \(\widehat{\beta}_{-S_k}\) is the full-refit target coefficient after deleting the selected set. Independent null simulations provide the reference distribution for \(T_k\). For each environment, defined by sample size, predictor distribution, and error distribution, the \(95\%\) cutoff is obtained from independent draws of the correctly specified data-generating process. The evaluation draws are not used to determine this cutoff. This calibration requires knowledge of the null data-generating process and is therefore available only in simulation. The results therefore characterise the detection performance of fixed-\(k\) MIS under a known null distribution. They do not establish the performance of a test directly applicable to observed data. For each mechanism--environment combination, the detection boundary is the smallest simulated severity at which the fitted rejection probability reaches \(80\%\). Combinations that do not reach this criterion within the simulated severity range are treated as right-censored. The remaining deletion fractions are treated as alternative sensitivity budgets rather than estimates of an underlying influential-set size.

The strength of concentrated coefficient sensitivity varies sharply across misspecification mechanisms. At the primary deletion fraction, nonlinear confounding, heterogeneous slopes, nonlinear functional form, and threshold behaviour reached the \(80\%\) detection criterion in all \(96\) environments, while the omitted-variable design reached it in \(8.3\%\) of environments. Linear endogeneity did not reach the criterion within the simulated severity range. These patterns show that the emergence of strongly concentrated coefficient sensitivity is mechanism specific under the stated deletion budget.

A complementary respecification check supports interpreting these responses as specification dependent. For the five mechanisms with an explicit correctly respecified model, each corrected specification is evaluated against its own independently calibrated null distribution. At \(k/n=0.025\), the false-positive rates under the corrected specifications range from \(4.9\%\) to \(5.1\%\), close to the nominal \(5\%\) level. Under the corresponding misspecified models, rejection rates range from \(11.1\%\) for the omitted-variable design to \(81.9\%\) for nonlinear functional form. The concentrated sensitivity signal therefore largely disappears after the relevant model departure is incorporated into the fitted specification. The weak omitted-variable response remains an important exception on the misspecified side rather than a calibration failure after correction (\autoref{tab:05-specificity}).

\autoref{fig:05-boundary-by-n} shows how the severity required to attain the \(80\%\) criterion varies with sample size. For nonlinear confounding, heterogeneous slopes, missing interactions, nonlinear functional form, and threshold behaviour, the required severity generally falls as sample size increases. Heteroskedasticity shows the same pattern beyond the smallest design.

Changing \(k/n\) changes the set over which influence is optimised and therefore changes the operating characteristics of the diagnostic. The simulated deletion fractions are interpreted as alternative fixed-budget sensitivity analyses; their relative performance does not identify a uniquely correct influential-set size.

Linear endogeneity identifies a formal limit of coefficient-targeted deletion sensitivity in this design.

\begin{proposition}[Fitted-span invariance]
	\label{prop:span-invariance}
	Let \(D\) denote the fitted design matrix and suppose that, across severity levels \(\lambda\), the outcome can be written as
	\[
	\mathbf{y}_{\lambda}
	=
	D\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\lambda}
	+
	b_{\lambda}\mathbf{v},
	\qquad b_{\lambda}\neq0,
	\]
	where \(D\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) do not depend on \(\lambda\). Then, for every admissible deletion set \(S\), the absolute deletion effect and the full-sample standard error scale by the same factor \(|b_{\lambda}|\). Consequently, the standardised deletion-sensitivity objective is invariant to \(\lambda\), and the set of its maximisers is unchanged across severity levels.
\end{proposition}

The proof and the corresponding full-refit result are given in \autoref{app:deriv-invariance}. The simulated linear-endogeneity design satisfies the fitted-span representation in \autoref{prop:span-invariance}: increasing severity changes the coefficient representation while leaving the standardised deletion-sensitivity structure unchanged. The MIS statistic is consequently invariant up to numerical precision across severity levels. This result does not imply an absence of endogeneity or coefficient distortion; rather, it shows that coefficient bias and concentrated deletion sensitivity are distinct properties. A deletion-based diagnostic need not respond when the misspecification component is absorbed entirely through the fitted span. Nonlinear confounding breaks this structure and produces a strong sensitivity response across the recorded environments.

Localisation at \(k/n=0.025\) differs substantially across the two mechanisms with a well-defined affected subset (\autoref{tab:05-localisation}). The threshold design shows clear enrichment of affected observations in the MIS-selected set, whereas heterogeneous slopes remain close to the chance/no-enrichment benchmark. A mechanism can therefore generate substantial target-coefficient sensitivity without causing the selected MIS observations to coincide closely with a nominal affected subgroup.

Post-deletion refitting can leave the underlying misspecification unresolved and can increase coefficient error. \autoref{fig:05-post-deletion-rmse} shows that coefficient RMSE can rise as the deletion budget grows after the selected observations are removed and the model is refitted. Deletion changes the estimating sample, while model correction requires changes to the maintained specification or identifying assumptions. The result gives localisation a diagnostic interpretation: it identifies where finite-sample coefficient sensitivity is concentrated within the maintained specification.

The misspecification study establishes a second source of concentrated target-coefficient sensitivity. \autoref{fig:05-boundary-by-n} shows that its strength depends sharply on the form of the model departure, while \autoref{tab:05-localisation} shows that the composition of the selected set can vary independently of the overall sensitivity signal. \autoref{fig:05-post-deletion-rmse} further shows that deleting increasingly large selected sets need not improve coefficient estimation, giving localisation a diagnostic rather than automatic cleaning interpretation within the maintained specification. Taken together, the three simulations show that concentrated target-coefficient sensitivity is mechanism dependent, that localisation and estimator stability are separate statistical objects, and that concentrated sensitivity can arise through both contamination and model misspecification. The empirical analysis next examines the magnitude and structure of this sensitivity across realised estimation samples.
