\section{Simulation Results}
\label{sec:simulation}

This section evaluates the MIS framework through three substantive simulation studies. The first examines influential-set recovery under distributional contamination, the second compares post-deletion and direct robust estimation, and the third examines coefficient sensitivity under model misspecification. Algorithmic, computational, and EVT validation is reported separately in \autoref{app:numerical} because it supports the methods used in these studies rather than defining an additional substantive experiment.

\subsection{Influential-Set Recovery under Distributional Contamination}
\label{subsec:detection-performance}

The first simulation study evaluates how fixed-\(k\) MIS recovers injected observations under contamination mechanisms with different implications for the target slope coefficient. Cook's distance, leverage, and DFBETAS provide observation-level comparators that respond to changes in fitted values, covariate extremeness, and single-observation coefficient sensitivity. MIS instead selects a size-\(k\) subset that maximises the change in the target coefficient. Recovery against the injected set therefore measures how closely each diagnostic objective aligns with the mechanism generating the contaminated observations.

The design varies sample size \(n\in\{500,1000,2500,5000\}\) and contamination proportion \(c\in\{0.005,0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}\). The injected-set size is
\[
k=\max\{\lfloor cn\rfloor,2\}.
\]
The simulation therefore supplies oracle \(k\). For contaminated samples, the known injected set is also used to resolve the directional ambiguity of the fixed-\(k\) search. Observation identities are not supplied to the subsequent search itself. The resulting procedure is therefore an oracle-assisted localisation benchmark for both set size and direction. It is referred to below as MIS with oracle \(k\) and oracle direction.

The three contamination mechanisms differ in how they affect the target slope. Response outliers shift the outcome at ordinary covariate positions, while good-leverage observations move far into predictor space but remain approximately aligned with the regression relation. Bad leverage combines both features: high leverage is paired with a response shift that rotates the fitted slope. All methods are evaluated against the same injected set.

Bad leverage produced the clearest alignment between the MIS objective and the injected mechanism. In the equal-weight design-cell summary, MIS recovered \(92.9\%\) of the injected observations, compared with \(86.5\%\) for the highest-recovery classical diagnostic (\autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary}). Across the sample-size--contamination grid, the target-aware recovery difference remained positive and ranged from \(8.0\) to \(16.0\) percentage points (\autoref{fig:02-mis-advantage}). \autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary} always uses the highest classical recovery, while the figure uses the lowest classical benchmark for the two negative-control mechanisms. Bad leverage therefore provides the setting in which the injected set is most closely aligned with the coefficient-targeted MIS objective.

Response outliers produce the opposite recovery pattern. MIS recovered \(26.6\%\) of the injected observations, compared with \(48.4\%\) for the highest-recovery classical diagnostic (\autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary}). Here the classical procedures benefit from residual extremeness, while a large residual need not create strong target-slope influence. MIS therefore recovers fewer injected observations because its objective is tied to coefficient movement.

Good leverage produces a larger recovery gap. The highest-recovery classical diagnostic recovered \(70.1\%\) of the injected observations, while MIS recovered \(36.9\%\) (\autoref{tab:02-detection-power-summary}). The injected observations are extreme in predictor space but remain approximately aligned with the fitted relation, so covariate extremeness need not translate into strong slope influence. This distinction motivates the separate mechanism diagnostic below.

A separate mechanism diagnostic examines this result at \(n=1000\), \(k=10\), with normal predictors and \(200\) Monte Carlo iterations per error distribution. MIS-selected observations typically had much lower leverage but substantially larger residual and absolute-DFBETA magnitudes than the injected good-leverage set (\autoref{fig:02-good-leverage-mechanism} and \autoref{tab:02-good-leverage-mechanism}). Under normal errors, for example, the median leverage ratio was \(0.07\), while the corresponding absolute-DFBETA and residual ratios were \(19.19\) and \(131.91\). The diagnostic therefore tracks coefficient influence more closely than covariate extremeness.

The distributional results preserve this mechanism-dependent interpretation while identifying conditions in which the separation weakens. Under bad leverage, MIS recovery exceeded \(97\%\) for normal, Beta\((2,5)\), mixed-normal, skewed-\(t\), and Pareto errors, but fell to \(78.5\%\) under GPD errors (\autoref{fig:02-distributional-robustness}). The simulated GPD shape parameters are \(\xi\in\{1.5,3,5,10\}\), for which the population mean does not exist. This condition is therefore treated separately as an extreme stress design. Auxiliary MIS-EVT calibration and convergence results are reported in \autoref{app:detection-evt-diagnostics}.

Overall, fixed-\(k\) MIS is most closely aligned with the injected set when the contamination mechanism creates concentrated joint influence on the target coefficient. Lower recovery under response-outlier and good-leverage contamination does not indicate failure of the coefficient-targeted diagnostic, because the injected set need not be the set that most strongly perturbs the target slope. The next simulation asks a separate question: whether successful localisation of coefficient-relevant observations translates into improved post-deletion coefficient estimation.











\subsection{Robust Estimation and Influential-Set Recovery}
\label{subsec:estimation-performance}

The second study separates influential-set localisation from coefficient estimation. It compares full-sample OLS, three classical diagnostic-removal procedures, fixed-\(k\) MIS, and two direct robust estimators. Leverage, Cook's distance, DFBETAS, and MIS select observations before an OLS refit, whereas MM regression and LTS modify estimation directly. The distinction matters because successful localisation and stable coefficient estimation need not move together.

The formal design varies sample size \(n\in\{500,1000,2500,5000\}\) and contamination proportion \(c\in\{0.005,0.01,0.025,0.05\}\), with three predictor environments and eight error distributions. The predictor designs are normal, a two-component normal mixture, and a contaminated-normal design; these differ from the predictor families used in the preceding distributional-recovery study. The full experiment contains \(1{,}248\) design cells with \(100\) Monte Carlo iterations per cell. Primary summaries exclude the infinite-mean GPD stress condition and give equal weight to the resulting finite-moment design cells.

The MIS benchmark conditions on two oracle quantities: the injected-set size and the direction of the target-coefficient perturbation induced by the injected set. Conditional on these quantities, the identities of the selected observations are determined by the Dinkelbach-based fixed-\(k\) search. The benchmark therefore isolates influential-set localisation from the separate problems of choosing \(k\) and choosing the search direction. Under no contamination, oracle \(k=0\), so MIS coincides with full-sample OLS by construction. The corresponding design and oracle checks are reported in \autoref{tab:robust-design-audit} and \autoref{tab:robust-oracle-audit}.

Direct robust estimators produced the smallest aggregate coefficient errors in the finite-moment comparison. Under bad leverage, full-sample OLS had mean absolute bias \(1.903\) and RMSE \(2.594\), while MIS with oracle \(k\) reduced these quantities to \(0.076\) and \(0.168\), respectively. Cook's distance and DFBETAS also improved substantially on OLS, but their corresponding mean absolute biases were \(0.296\) and \(0.242\). MM and LTS remained more accurate still, with bad-leverage mean absolute biases of \(0.007\) and \(0.008\).

The pooled results conceal substantial predictor-distribution heterogeneity. Under bad leverage, OLS mean absolute bias ranged from \(3.533\) with normal predictors to \(0.374\) with contaminated-normal predictors. The relative ordering of the deletion procedures also changes across these environments: MIS compares favourably with Cook's distance and DFBETAS under normal and normal-mixture predictors, whereas the ordering reverses under the contaminated-normal predictor design (\autoref{tab:robust-bad-leverage-by-predictor} and \autoref{fig:04A-mis-oracle-vs-classical}). The contaminated-normal predictor design can generate extreme-leverage observations through the background predictor distribution itself, so the injected set is not necessarily the only source of target-coefficient sensitivity. Post-deletion accuracy therefore depends on the geometry of the observations that remain.

The coefficient-error distributions reinforce the distinction between aggregate accuracy and dispersion. The infinite-mean GPD design is excluded from this primary figure and treated separately as a stress condition rather than as a regular finite-moment sampling regime.

The wide MIS distributions under response-outlier and good-leverage contamination do not conflict with the recovery results in the preceding study. The injected set can be unusual without materially distorting the slope. In the present study, MIS is nevertheless required to select and delete exactly \(k\) observations. When little coefficient distortion needs repair, selecting the size-\(k\) set that maximises coefficient movement can perturb an otherwise stable estimate. Bad leverage differs because the injected set itself generates substantial slope distortion, bringing localisation and post-deletion estimation into closer alignment.

Coverage requires separate interpretation for direct estimators and deletion-based procedures. Under bad leverage, MM retained \(95.0\%\) coverage, MIS with oracle \(k\) recorded \(78.1\%\), and full-sample OLS fell to \(9.6\%\) (\autoref{tab:robust-coverage}). The remaining diagnostic-deletion procedures showed similarly intermediate coverage. Because these intervals are ordinary OLS intervals computed after data-dependent deletion, their reported coverage describes interval behaviour after selection without adjustment for that selection step. Good leverage shows a different pattern: MM and LTS retained small point-estimation error while their coverage fell to \(69.5\%\) and \(65.5\%\). Point accuracy and interval calibration can therefore move separately.

\input{../../output/04_robust/tables/main/04_tab2c_coverage.tex}

Influential-set localisation reveals the diagnostic information provided by fixed-\(k\) MIS. Under bad leverage, MIS recovered \(96.0\%\) of the injected set while selecting exactly the oracle set size (\autoref{tab:robust-detection-efficiency}). Cook's distance and DFBETAS achieved slightly higher recall, at \(98.2\%\) and \(98.4\%\), but their mean selection-size ratios were \(3.19\) and \(3.32\). Their corresponding precisions were \(43.9\%\) and \(42.3\%\), compared with \(96.0\%\) for MIS. Leverage selected almost five times the injected-set size while recovering \(67.7\%\) of it.

Because MIS selects exactly \(k\) observations when the injected set also has size \(k\), its precision and recall coincide; both represent injected-set recovery. Recovery is much lower under response-outlier and good-leverage contamination, consistent with the narrower coefficient-targeted objective. The oracle audit helps explain this contrast: the mean absolute coefficient shift produced by deleting the true injected set is \(1.902\) under bad leverage, compared with \(0.020\) for response outliers and \(0.003\) for good leverage.

The second study therefore separates three statistical objectives. MM and LTS provide the strongest aggregate point-estimation performance, classical deletion diagnostics can obtain high recall by selecting substantially larger sets, and fixed-\(k\) MIS provides a more selective coefficient-targeted localisation benchmark under bad leverage. High injected-set recovery need not produce the smallest post-deletion coefficient error because the refitted coefficient also depends on the geometry of the remaining sample. Both studies so far introduce contamination into an otherwise correctly specified regression model; the final study asks how coefficient sensitivity behaves when the specification itself is wrong.


\subsection{Model Misspecification and Diagnostic Sensitivity}
\label{subsec:misspecification}

\subsubsection{Design and Calibration}
\label{subsubsec:misspec-design}

The third simulation study characterises when coefficient-targeted deletion sensitivity responds to model misspecification. Eight mechanisms are considered: linear endogeneity, nonlinear confounding, heterogeneous slopes, heteroskedasticity, a missing interaction, nonlinear functional form, an omitted variable, and threshold behaviour. Each scenario is evaluated in \(96\) environments formed by four sample sizes, three predictor distributions, and eight error distributions, giving \(8\times96=768\) scenario--environment combinations.

MIS is compared with Cook's distance, DFBETAS, leverage, Ramsey's RESET, and the Breusch--Pagan test. The principal deletion fraction is \(k/n=0.025\), with \(0.01\), \(0.05\), and \(0.10\) used for sensitivity analysis. Detector cutoffs come from an independent null-calibration stream.

For MIS, diagnostic sensitivity is measured by
\[
T_k
=
\frac{
	\left|
	\widehat{\beta}_{-S_k}-\widehat{\beta}
	\right|
}{
	\widehat{\operatorname{se}}(\widehat{\beta})
},
\]
where \(\widehat{\beta}\) is the full-sample estimate of the target coefficient, \(\widehat{\beta}_{-S_k}\) is the corresponding estimate after deleting \(S_k\), and \(S_k\) is the size-\(k\) set producing the larger absolute target-coefficient movement across the two directional searches. The denominator places coefficient movement on the uncertainty scale of the original fit. Its role is scale normalisation. Inference uses the empirical null calibration, with no Gaussian or \(t\) reference distribution assumed. Larger values indicate greater target-coefficient sensitivity, and rejection occurs when \(T_k\) exceeds the corresponding empirically calibrated null cutoff.

For the five additive mechanisms, \(\lambda\) measures the standard-deviation ratio of the imposed misspecification component to the baseline error. Heteroskedasticity and the two confounding designs use mechanism-specific severity scales. Empirical size is rejection at zero severity; detection reach records whether the fitted monotone power curve reaches \(80\%\), and the detection boundary records the corresponding severity when it does. Detailed calibration and environment-level results are reported in \autoref{app:misspecification-results}.

\subsubsection{Calibration and Detection Breadth}
\label{subsubsec:misspec-breadth}


The independent calibration produces rejection rates close to the nominal \(5\%\) level under the reference model. MIS ranges from \(5.04\%\) to \(5.07\%\) across deletion fractions, and the competing procedures are similarly calibrated (\autoref{tab:05-size-by-k}). At \(k/n=0.025\), MIS reached \(80\%\) power in \(551\) of the \(768\) scenario--environment combinations, or \(71.7\%\), compared with \(68.4\%\) for Breusch--Pagan and \(50.0\%\) for RESET (\autoref{fig:05-detection-reach} and \autoref{tab:05-detection-reach}). The observation-level influence diagnostics reached substantially fewer environments. With baseline rejection rates aligned, these differences describe variation in detection breadth across misspecification mechanisms.

\subsubsection{Detection Boundary and the Role of \(k\)}
\label{subsubsec:misspec-boundary}

The severity required for reliable MIS detection is strongly mechanism dependent. At \(k/n=0.025\), nonlinear confounding, heterogeneous slopes, nonlinearity, and threshold behaviour reached \(80\%\) power in all \(96\) environments. Linear endogeneity never reached the criterion, while the omitted-variable design did so in only \(8.3\%\) (\autoref{tab:05-boundary-summary}). Among the additive mechanisms with full reach, the median boundary ranged from \(0.209\) for nonlinearity to \(0.307\) for threshold behaviour.

\input{../../output/05_misspecification/tables/main/05_tab_boundary_summary.tex}

Increasing sample size generally lowers the severity required for detection when the MIS statistic responds to the structural departure (\autoref{fig:05-boundary-by-n}). This decline appears for nonlinear confounding, heterogeneous slopes, missing interactions, nonlinearity, threshold behaviour, and, after the smallest design, heteroskedasticity. Linear endogeneity and the omitted-variable design remain the exceptions. Neither attains the \(80\%\) criterion at any displayed sample size in the pooled comparison.

The deletion fraction changes the operating characteristics without identifying a unique preferred value. Pooled reach was highest at \(k/n=0.025\), at \(71.7\%\), and fell to \(52.2\%\) at \(k/n=0.10\) (\autoref{fig:05-budget-reach} and \autoref{tab:05-budget-summary}). The censoring-aware boundary shows the same broad pattern. These fixed deletion fractions therefore provide a sensitivity analysis of the detector's operating behaviour.

\subsubsection{Specificity, Invariance, and Localisation}
\label{subsubsec:misspec-specificity}

The wrong and correctly specified model classes require separate null calibration because the null distribution of \(T_k\) depends on the fitted specification. At \(k/n=0.025\), four wrong specifications produced rejection between \(62.9\%\) and \(81.9\%\), while the omitted-variable design was much weaker at \(11.1\%\) (\autoref{fig:05-specificity} and \autoref{tab:05-specificity}). After each correctly specified model was calibrated against its own null distribution, rejection returned to approximately \(5\%\). The substantive contrast is therefore the persistence of strong rejection for four wrong specifications after model-class-specific calibration.

Linear endogeneity is an algebraic fitted-span invariance in this design, not an ordinary low-power case. The endogeneity component is linear in the fitted regressor, so changing its severity changes the coefficient representation without changing the relevant deletion-sensitivity ordering. The maximum recorded discrepancy in the standardised MIS statistic across matched severity levels was only \(1.87\times10^{-13}\), confirming the invariance numerically (\autoref{tab:05-invariance-blind-set}). The corresponding \(4.95\%\) rejection rate is therefore a consequence of null calibration rather than evidence used to establish the invariance. The associated lack of rejection is not unique to MIS in this design: the competing diagnostics also remain near their calibrated null rates. The algebraic invariance established above applies specifically to the MIS deletion-sensitivity statistic. The invariance persists across all four deletion fractions, so varying \(k\)
within the simulated range does not alter this result.

\input{../../output/05_misspecification/tables/main/05_tab07_invariance_blind_set.tex}

\input{../../output/05_misspecification/tables/main/05_invariance_argument.tex}

The nonlinear-confounding experiment establishes the scope of this invariance. Linear endogeneity did not attain \(80\%\) power in any recorded environment at any deletion fraction. Nonlinear confounding, which replaces the linear-in-\(X\) component with a standardised quadratic component, attained \(80\%\) power in all recorded environments at deletion fractions of \(1\%\), \(2.5\%\), and \(5\%\). The contrast locates the invariance within the fitted linear span. Nonlinear confounding outside that span produces strong deletion sensitivity.

Localisation is defined only for mechanisms with a well-defined observation-level affected subset. Heterogeneous slopes identify the \(G_i=1\) subgroup, which comprises approximately \(25\%\) of observations, while threshold misspecification identifies observations above the threshold. Nonlinearity, missing interaction, and omitted-variable misspecification do not define an analogous binary affected set, so precision and lift are not reported for those mechanisms.

At \(k/n=0.025\), threshold misspecification produced precision \(0.606\) and lift \(2.43\) relative to the \(25\%\) affected fraction, indicating substantial enrichment among the MIS-selected observations (\autoref{tab:05-localisation}). Heterogeneous slopes produced precision \(0.274\) against an affected fraction of approximately \(0.25\), with lift \(1.10\). The latter result lies close to the no-enrichment benchmark. Because no Monte Carlo interval is reported for this contrast, the small difference from \(0.25\) is interpreted descriptively. The localisation contrast indicates that enrichment is strongest when the misspecification is concentrated on an identifiable subset of observations.

\subsubsection{Deletion Consequences and Interpretation}
\label{subsubsec:misspec-repair}

Identifying observations associated with misspecification does not imply that deleting them restores a correctly specified model. Relative to the structural target coefficient used in the simulation, the lower panels of \autoref{fig:05-targeting-localisation} show that coefficient RMSE can increase as the deletion fraction grows. The relevant comparison is the within-panel change across deletion fractions; absolute RMSE levels should not be compared directly across predictor environments because the panels use different vertical scales. Deleting observations changes the estimating sample. It does not add an omitted variable, restore exogeneity, or supply a missing nonlinear or interaction term.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../../output/05_misspecification/figures/main/05_fig06_targeting_localisation.pdf}
	\caption{Localisation and deletion consequences. The upper panels report localisation lift and precision for heterogeneous-slope and threshold designs, with diamonds marking the chance/reference level. The lower panels report mean cell-level RMSE for the full-sample and diagnostic-deletion estimators across predictor environments and fixed deletion fractions.}
	\label{fig:05-targeting-localisation}
\end{figure}

Taken together, the misspecification study identifies where fixed-\(k\) MIS is informative. It responds strongly when misspecification creates concentrated target-coefficient sensitivity, remains invariant to departures contained within the fitted linear span, and can localise affected observations when the disturbance is concentrated on a defined subgroup. The diagnostic therefore characterises coefficient sensitivity; structural model repair requires changing the specification itself.
