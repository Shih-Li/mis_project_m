\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%---background---
Empirical regression coefficients can depend strongly on small subsets of the estimation sample. Classical case-deletion diagnostics provide a direct way to study this dependence one observation at a time \parencite{cook2000detection,belsley2005regression}. However, single-case diagnostics can become less informative when influence is distributed across several observations. Under masking, deleting observations individually may produce only modest coefficient changes, even when deleting them jointly produces a much larger change \parencite{rousseeuw1990unmasking,hadi1993procedures}. Recent data-dropping methods have renewed attention to this broader sensitivity problem by examining how empirical conclusions change when selected groups of observations are removed \parencite{broderick2020automatic,kuschnig2021hidden}.

Outlyingness and target-coefficient influence are related but distinct properties. An observation can be unusual because of its outcome, covariates, or relationship to the fitted model while exerting limited influence on a prespecified regression coefficient. Conversely, substantial coefficient movement can arise from a group of observations that is not conventionally classified as an outlier set. This thesis uses \emph{concentrated target-coefficient sensitivity} as a descriptive term for cases in which deleting a relatively small subset of observations produces substantial movement in the coefficient of interest. The question is therefore not only which observations are unusual, but also how coefficient sensitivity is distributed across the estimation sample and where it is concentrated.

Most Influential Sets (MIS) provide a coefficient-targeted framework for studying this structure. For a prespecified deletion budget \(k\), fixed-\(k\) MIS searches jointly for the size-\(k\) set that maximises movement in a target regression coefficient \parencite{kuschnig2021hidden,hu2024most,konrad2026finding}. The resulting set characterises joint influence under a stated coefficient target, fitted specification, and deletion budget. This perspective differs from robust estimation, which modifies the estimation rule to obtain coefficient stability under contamination, and from specification testing, which evaluates particular implications of model departures. These distinctions make it possible to study localisation, estimation, and model misspecification as related but separate aspects of coefficient sensitivity.

Existing work provides methods for identifying influential observation sets and worst-case deletion sensitivity. However, the statistical interpretation of the resulting sets remains less settled. A set that maximises movement in a target coefficient need not coincide with residual outliers, high-leverage observations, or observations downweighted by a robust estimator. Concentrated target-coefficient sensitivity can also arise under model misspecification, but the resulting influential set does not have a single diagnostic interpretation. Fixed-\(k\) MIS therefore provides a distinct localisation perspective by identifying where finite-sample dependence of a prespecified coefficient is concentrated. This thesis examines how that perspective relates to outlyingness, leverage, estimator stability, model misspecification, and realised empirical applications.

Accordingly, the overarching research question is: \emph{What does fixed-\(k\) MIS reveal about how target-coefficient sensitivity arises, where it is concentrated, and how it appears in empirical applications?}

This overarching question is developed through four subquestions:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \emph{Under different contamination mechanisms, when do outliers coincide with the set carrying the greatest joint target-coefficient influence?}
	
	\item \emph{How does localisation of joint target-coefficient influence relate to coefficient stability under robust estimation?}
	
	\item \emph{How does model misspecification generate concentrated target-coefficient sensitivity, and how does the resulting influential-set structure depend on the form of misspecification?}
	
	\item \emph{How do the magnitude and structure of target-coefficient sensitivity vary across empirical applications?}
\end{enumerate}

The four subquestions develop a common interpretation of fixed-\(k\) MIS in stages. The first asks when coefficient-targeted localisation coincides with outlyingness under different contamination mechanisms. The second examines whether the same localisation corresponds to coefficient stability under robust estimation. The third extends the source of concentrated target-coefficient sensitivity from planted contamination to model misspecification and examines how its influential-set structure changes across model departures. The fourth carries this interpretation into realised empirical samples by examining how strongly and where target-coefficient sensitivity is concentrated. The first three questions correspond respectively to \autoref{subsec:detection-performance}, \autoref{subsec:estimation-performance}, and \autoref{subsec:misspecification}, while the fourth is examined through the empirical audits in \autoref{sec:application}.

The simulations show that the relationship between conventional unusualness and target-coefficient influence is strongly mechanism dependent. The three contamination mechanisms differ in how the planted observations depart from the fitted relationship: response outliers shift the outcome at ordinary predictor positions, good-leverage observations occupy extreme predictor positions while remaining close to the fitted relation, and bad-leverage observations are displaced in both predictor and outcome space. Bad leverage creates close alignment between planted contamination and target-slope influence, whereas response outliers and good leverage produce weaker correspondence between unusualness and coefficient movement. Localisation and coefficient accuracy also need not coincide: identifying the set that maximises coefficient movement addresses a different statistical objective from obtaining a stable estimator under contamination. Model departures generate different patterns of concentrated target-coefficient sensitivity and influential-set localisation. Several model departures produce strong concentrated sensitivity. In the linear-endogeneity design, the fitted-span structure produces an invariance boundary: increasing severity changes the coefficient representation without changing the standardised deletion-sensitivity structure.

The empirical analysis examines the magnitude and structure of the same sensitivity across ten published studies built around randomized experimental variation. For each study, the analysis evaluates one prespecified estimand under a common deletion protocol with full-refit validation. The audits show substantial cross-study heterogeneity in the deletion fraction required to produce large coefficient movements. They also reveal different influential-set structures, including persistent influential cores, membership turnover across deletion budgets, and geographic concentration. These results characterise finite-sample dependence of the audited coefficients within their validated specifications.

The thesis contributes an interpretation of fixed-\(k\) MIS as a coefficient-targeted localisation device. First, the contamination simulations show that alignment between conventional outlyingness and joint target-coefficient influence is mechanism specific: bad leverage produces close alignment, whereas response outliers and good leverage can be unusual without forming the most influential fixed-\(k\) set. Second, the robust-estimation comparison separates influential-set localisation from estimator stability by showing that deleting the maximally influential set can either reduce or increase coefficient error depending on the contamination mechanism. Third, the misspecification analysis shows that concentrated sensitivity can arise without planted contamination, documents model-specific localisation patterns, and establishes a fitted-span invariance boundary under linear endogeneity; correctly respecified models return the calibrated MIS rejection rate to approximately nominal size. Finally, the empirical audits apply a common fixed-\(k\) protocol to ten published experimental studies and document substantial heterogeneity in both the magnitude of coefficient movement and the composition of influential sets.

The remainder of the thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews classical influence diagnostics, joint influence, data-dropping methods, robust regression, and model misspecification. Section 3 defines the fixed-\(k\) MIS methodology, including the set-DFBETA, linear-fractional optimisation, statistical calibration, and the role of the deletion budget. Section 4 presents the simulation evidence on outlyingness, influence localisation and estimation, and misspecification-induced coefficient sensitivity. Section 5 reports cross-study empirical evidence on the magnitude and structure of target-coefficient sensitivity. Section 6 discusses the findings, limitations, and future development of influential-set analysis, while Section 7 concludes.
