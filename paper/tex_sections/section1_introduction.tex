\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%---background---
Empirical regression coefficients can depend strongly on relatively small subsets of the estimation sample. Classical case-deletion diagnostics provide a direct way to study this dependence one observation at a time \parencite{cook2000detection,belsley2005regression}. Their observation-level structure can become less informative when influence is distributed across several observations, particularly under masking and related forms of joint influence \parencite{rousseeuw1990unmasking,hadi1993procedures}. Recent data-dropping methods have renewed attention to this broader sensitivity problem by asking how empirical conclusions change when selected groups of observations are removed \parencite{broderick2020automatic,kuschnig2021hidden}.

Outlyingness and target-coefficient influence are related but distinct properties. An observation can be unusual because of its outcome, covariates, or relationship to the fitted model while exerting limited influence on a prespecified regression coefficient. Conversely, substantial coefficient movement can arise from a group of observations that is not conventionally classified as an outlier set. This thesis refers descriptively to \emph{concentrated target-coefficient sensitivity} when deletion of a relatively small subset produces substantial movement in the coefficient of interest. The relevant question is therefore not only which observations are unusual, but how coefficient sensitivity becomes distributed and concentrated across the estimation sample.

Most Influential Sets (MIS) provide a coefficient-targeted framework for studying this structure. For a prespecified deletion budget \(k\), fixed-\(k\) MIS searches jointly for the size-\(k\) set that maximises movement in a target regression coefficient \parencite{kuschnig2021hidden,hu2024most,konrad2026finding}. The resulting set characterises joint influence under a stated coefficient target, fitted specification, and deletion budget. This perspective differs from robust estimation, which modifies the estimation rule to obtain coefficient stability under contamination, and from specification testing, which evaluates particular implications of model departures. These distinctions make it possible to study localisation, estimation, and model misspecification as related but separate aspects of coefficient sensitivity.

Existing work provides the machinery for identifying influential observation sets and worst-case deletion sensitivity, but the diagnostic implications of MIS have not been systematically separated across several empirically distinct settings. In particular, it remains useful to distinguish whether influential sets coincide with conventionally unusual observations, how influence localisation relates to coefficient stability, how model misspecification appears through concentrated deletion sensitivity, and how these patterns manifest in applied empirical settings. This thesis studies these questions separately within a common fixed-\(k\) MIS framework.

Accordingly, the overarching research question is: \emph{What does fixed-\(k\) MIS reveal about how target-coefficient sensitivity arises, where it is concentrated, and how it appears in empirical applications?}

This overarching question is developed through four subquestions:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \emph{Under different contamination mechanisms, when do conventionally unusual observations coincide with the observations carrying the greatest joint influence on the target coefficient?}
	
	\item \emph{How does localisation of joint target-coefficient influence relate to post-deletion coefficient estimation and to robust estimators designed for coefficient stability?}
	
	\item \emph{Can model misspecification generate target-coefficient sensitivity without planted contamination, and how does the resulting influential-set structure depend on the form of misspecification?}
	
	\item \emph{How do the magnitude and structure of target-coefficient sensitivity vary across empirical applications?}
\end{enumerate}

The subquestions separate four aspects of the broader sensitivity problem. The first examines the relationship between conventional outlyingness and coefficient-targeted joint influence under different contamination mechanisms. The second distinguishes localisation of influential observations from the separate objective of obtaining stable coefficient estimates. The third extends the analysis beyond planted contamination by examining sensitivity generated by model misspecification. The fourth evaluates how the magnitude and composition of influential sets vary across realised empirical samples. The first three questions correspond respectively to \autoref{subsec:detection-performance}, \autoref{subsec:estimation-performance}, and \autoref{subsec:misspecification}, while the fourth is examined through the empirical audits in \autoref{sec:application}.

The simulations show that the structure of coefficient sensitivity is strongly mechanism dependent. Bad leverage creates close alignment between planted contamination and target-slope influence, while response outliers and good leverage produce weaker correspondence between unusualness and coefficient movement. Localisation and coefficient accuracy also need not coincide: identifying the set that maximises coefficient movement addresses a different statistical objective from obtaining a stable estimator under contamination. The misspecification results extend this distinction beyond planted outliers. Several model departures generate strong concentrated sensitivity, while the fitted-span structure of the linear-endogeneity design produces an invariance boundary in which severity changes the coefficient representation without changing the standardised deletion-sensitivity structure.

The empirical analysis examines the magnitude and structure of the same sensitivity across ten published studies built around randomized experimental variation. One prespecified estimand from each study is evaluated under a common deletion protocol with full-refit validation. The audits show substantial cross-study heterogeneity in the deletion fraction required to produce large coefficient movements. They also reveal different influential-set structures, including persistent influential cores, membership turnover across deletion budgets, and geographic concentration. These results characterise finite-sample dependence of the audited coefficients within their validated specifications.

The thesis makes four contributions within this coefficient-sensitivity framework. First, the contamination simulations show that alignment between conventional outlyingness and joint target-coefficient influence is mechanism specific: bad leverage produces close alignment, whereas response outliers and good leverage can be unusual without forming the most influential fixed-\(k\) set. Second, the robust-estimation comparison separates influential-set localisation from estimator stability by showing that deleting the maximally influential set can either reduce or increase coefficient error depending on the contamination mechanism. Third, the misspecification analysis shows that concentrated sensitivity can arise without planted contamination, documents model-specific localisation patterns, and establishes a fitted-span invariance boundary under linear endogeneity; correctly respecified models return the calibrated MIS rejection rate to approximately nominal size. Finally, the empirical audits apply a common fixed-\(k\) protocol to ten published experimental studies and document substantial heterogeneity in both the magnitude of coefficient movement and the composition of influential sets. These contributions use the existing fixed-\(k\) MIS machinery to characterise when, where, and with what consequences target-coefficient sensitivity becomes concentrated.

The remainder of the thesis proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews classical influence diagnostics, joint influence, data-dropping methods, robust regression, and model misspecification. Section 3 defines the fixed-\(k\) MIS methodology, including the set-DFBETA, linear-fractional optimisation, statistical calibration, and the role of the deletion budget. Section 4 presents the simulation evidence on outlyingness, influence localisation and estimation, and misspecification-induced coefficient sensitivity. Section 5 reports cross-study empirical evidence on the magnitude and structure of target-coefficient sensitivity. Section 6 concludes and discusses the scope and future development of influential-set analysis.
