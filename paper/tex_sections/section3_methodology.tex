\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section defines fixed-\(k\) MIS as a coefficient-targeted method for localising joint influence. The methodology isolates a prespecified regression coefficient, expresses its change under set deletion as a set-DFBETA, formulates the fixed-\(k\) subset-selection problem, and exploits its linear-fractional structure for global optimisation. Statistical calibration is treated separately from optimisation.

The deletion size \(k\) is prespecified and defines the sensitivity budget. Different values of \(k\) therefore pose different subset-deletion questions and can generate different influential sets. The simulation designs use either oracle-assisted values of \(k\) or analyst-specified deletion fractions, as described at the end of this section.

\subsection{Regression setup and target coefficient}
\label{subsec:regression_setup}

Consider the linear regression model
\begin{equation}
	y_i
	=
	x_{ij}\beta_j
	+
	\mathbf{z}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
	+
	\varepsilon_i,
	\qquad i\in[n].
	\label{eq:regression_target}
\end{equation}
The coefficient $\beta_j$ is the prespecified target of the influence analysis. Here, $[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ denotes the observation index set, $x_{ij}$ is the scalar target regressor, and $\mathbf{z}_i$ contains the remaining nuisance regressors with coefficient vector $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$. All deletion effects examined in this section therefore refer to changes in the estimate of $\beta_j$ within a fixed regression specification.

The Frisch--Waugh--Lovell theorem isolates the target coefficient by partialling the nuisance regressors out of the target regressor and the outcome \parencite{frisch1933partial,lovell1963seasonal}. Let $\widetilde{x}_i$ and $\widetilde{y}_i$ denote the resulting residualised target regressor and outcome, and write $\widehat{\beta}$ for the OLS estimate of the target coefficient $\beta_j$. The full-sample estimate is then
\begin{equation}
	\widehat{\beta}
	=
	\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{x}_i\widetilde{y}_i}
	{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{x}_i^2}.
	\label{eq:target_beta_residualised}
\end{equation}
This representation reduces estimation of the target coefficient to a scalar regression once the nuisance regressors have been partialled out. 

The search objective uses a fixed-residualised deletion convention. The residualised quantities $\widetilde{x}_i$ and $\widetilde{y}_i$ are computed on the full sample and held fixed when observations are deleted. The resulting leave-set-out coefficient is therefore obtained from the retained observations without re-estimating the nuisance projection. This convention defines the linear-fractional objective used below. Global optimisation through the Dinkelbach procedure applies to this fixed-residualised objective \parencite{konrad2026finding}.

Throughout, ``exact'' denotes global optimisation of the fixed-\(k\), fixed-residualised MIS objective. \emph{Full-refit validation} refers separately to re-estimating the original multivariate specification after deleting a selected set. The two coefficients can differ because full-refit validation re-estimates the nuisance projection. This terminology is used throughout the simulation and empirical analyses.

Define the corresponding residual of the residualised target regression by
\begin{equation}
	\widetilde{r}_i
	=
	\widetilde{y}_i-\widehat{\beta}\widetilde{x}_i.
	\label{eq:target_residual}
\end{equation}
These residuals record the variation in the residualised outcome that remains after estimation of the target coefficient, and by Frisch--Waugh--Lovell they coincide with the residuals of the full regression of $y_i$ on $x_{ij}$ and $\mathbf{z}_i$. For a deletion set $S\subseteq[n]$, let $\widehat{\beta}_{-S}$ denote the estimate obtained from the residualised regression over the retained observations $[n]\setminus S$. The difference between $\widehat{\beta}$ and $\widehat{\beta}_{-S}$ measures how the deleted observations shift the target coefficient. This difference depends on the deletion set as a whole and therefore defines influence at the level of the set rather than the individual observation.

\subsection{Set influence and set-DFBETA}
\label{subsec:set_influence}

For a deletion set \(S\subseteq[n]\), define the coefficient-targeted set influence by
\begin{equation}
	\Delta(S)
	=
	\widehat{\beta}
	-
	\widehat{\beta}_{-S}.
	\label{eq:set_dfbeta}
\end{equation}
This quantity is referred to throughout the thesis as the \emph{set-DFBETA}. Its sign records the direction of coefficient movement: a positive value indicates that deleting \(S\) lowers the target estimate, while a negative value indicates that deletion raises it.

Using the residualised variables defined in \autoref{subsec:regression_setup}, and substituting \autoref{eq:target_beta_residualised} and \autoref{eq:target_residual}, the set-DFBETA admits the closed-form representation
\begin{equation}
	\widehat{\beta}
	-
	\widehat{\beta}_{-S}
	=
	\frac{
		\sum_{s\in S}\widetilde{x}_s\widetilde{r}_s
	}{
		\sum_{t\notin S}\widetilde{x}_t^2
	}.
	\label{eq:set_dfbeta_ratio}
\end{equation}
The numerator collects the score contributions of the deleted observations. The denominator records the residualised design information remaining after deletion \parencite{konrad2026finding}. Set influence therefore depends jointly on the contribution removed from the sample and on the information retained outside the deletion set.

Set influence is a joint property of the selected observations. Changing the membership of $S$ alters the numerator and the denominator of \autoref{eq:set_dfbeta_ratio} at the same time. Each deleted observation also removes design information from the denominator. The set-DFBETA is therefore not generally equal to the sum of the individual DFBETAs of its members. This joint dependence provides one mechanism through which observation-level rankings can fail to reveal influential groups, including configurations associated with masking. It therefore motivates selecting deletion sets jointly rather than constructing them from individual diagnostic rankings. The following subsection formalises that selection problem for a fixed set size $k$.

\subsection{Fixed-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} most influential sets}
\label{subsec:fixed_k_mis}

For a prespecified deletion size \(k<n\), the fixed-\(k\) MIS is defined by
\begin{equation}
	S_k^*
	\in
	\arg\max_{\substack{S\subseteq[n]\\|S|=k}}
	|\Delta(S)|.
	\label{eq:absolute_fixed_k_influence}
\end{equation}
The absolute value allows influential movement in either coefficient direction. Computationally, the two directional problems can be solved separately and compared after optimisation.

Each prespecified value of \(k\) defines a separate deletion-budget question and can produce a different maximising set. Evaluating a sequence of values therefore traces how target-coefficient sensitivity and influential-set membership change with the deletion budget. The analysis does not treat \(k\) as an estimated contamination count.

Direct enumeration requires evaluation of \(\binom{n}{k}\) subsets. The closed-form set-DFBETA supplies additional structure because both its numerator and denominator are additive in set membership. This yields the linear-fractional optimisation problem used below.

\subsection{Linear-fractional reduction and Dinkelbach-based fixed-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} optimisation}
\label{subsec:dinkelbach_optimisation}

The leave-set-out representation in \autoref{eq:set_dfbeta_ratio} allows the fixed-$k$ influence problem to be written in a compact linear-fractional form. Define
\begin{equation}
	w_i := \widetilde{x}_i\widetilde{r}_i,
	\qquad
	c_i := \widetilde{x}_i^2,
	\qquad
	T := \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i,
	\label{eq:linear_fractional_components}
\end{equation}
and, for a deletion set $S$,
\begin{equation}
	W(S) := \sum_{i\in S} w_i,
	\qquad
	G(S) := T-\sum_{i\in S} c_i.
	\label{eq:WG_definitions}
\end{equation}
Here, $W(S)$ collects the deleted score contributions, while $G(S)$ measures the residualised design information remaining after deletion. The set-DFBETA in \autoref{eq:set_dfbeta_ratio} can therefore be written as
\begin{equation}
	\Delta(S)
	=
	\frac{W(S)}{G(S)}.
	\label{eq:linear_fractional_objective}
\end{equation}
The fixed-$k$ influence objective is therefore a ratio of two quantities that are additive in set membership.

The ratio in \autoref{eq:linear_fractional_objective} requires a positive denominator for every admissible deletion set. For a fixed set size $k$, the analysis therefore assumes

\begin{equation}
	G(S)>0
	\qquad
	\text{for all } S\subseteq[n] \text{ such that } |S|=k.
	\label{eq:positive_denominator}
\end{equation}
Under this condition, deleting any admissible size-\(k\) set leaves positive residualised information for estimating the target coefficient. For the positive direction, the fixed-\(k\) problem can be expressed as the linear-fractional optimisation problem.

\begin{equation}
	\max_{\substack{S\subseteq[n]\\|S|=k}}
	\frac{W(S)}{G(S)}.
	\label{eq:fixed_k_fractional_problem}
\end{equation}
The negative-direction problem is obtained by replacing \(W(S)\) with \(-W(S)\). The absolute-value MIS in \autoref{eq:absolute_fixed_k_influence} compares the optimised solutions from the two directions. This formulation preserves the joint dependence of the numerator and denominator on the selected deletion set \parencite{konrad2026finding}.

Under the positive-denominator condition, each directional fixed-\(k\) problem is a linear-fractional optimisation problem. For the positive direction and a Dinkelbach parameter \(\eta\), the transformed objective ranks observations by
\[
s_i(\eta)=w_i+\eta c_i.
\]
Conditional on \(\eta\), the maximising candidate consists of the \(k\) observations with the largest transformed scores. The ratio is then updated using \(\eta=W(S)/G(S)\), and the top-\(k\) problem is resolved. Under exact arithmetic and exact top-\(k\) selection, the sequence terminates finitely \parencite{dinkelbach1967nonlinear,konrad2026finding}.

For fixed residualised inputs and the positive-denominator condition in \autoref{eq:positive_denominator}, this procedure identifies a global maximiser of each directional fixed-\(k\) linear-fractional objective. Comparing the two directional optima therefore yields a global maximiser of the absolute fixed-\(k\) objective in \autoref{eq:absolute_fixed_k_influence}. This result provides the ``exact'' optimisation terminology defined in \autoref{subsec:regression_setup}. Numerical convergence and computational checks are reported in \autoref{app:numerical}.

\subsection{EVT-based inference for fixed-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} MIS}
\label{subsec:evt_fixed_k}

Optimisation and statistical calibration answer separate questions. Optimisation identifies the set that maximises the fixed-\(k\) objective, while calibration evaluates the magnitude of influence relative to a reference distribution. Because a maximum is selected over many overlapping deletion sets, its reference distribution differs from that of a prespecified set. The distributional-recovery simulation uses a block-maxima extreme-value construction as one calibration device \parencite{konrad2026testing}. In that simulation, the tested set is the injected set under contamination and a randomly selected size-\(k\) set under the clean design.

The block-maxima construction requires the deletion budget to remain sufficiently small relative to the observations available within each block. Detailed block-size restrictions, GEV diagnostics, numerical checks, and null-calibration results are reported in the appendices. EVT calibration remains conditional on the chosen \(k\); the misspecification simulation uses independently simulated empirical null distributions instead.

\subsection{Role of \texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} in the simulation designs}
\label{subsec:k_simulation_role}

The simulations use \(k\) in two distinct ways. In the distributional-recovery and robust-estimation studies, the data-generating process supplies an oracle deletion budget equal to the injected-set size. The distributional-recovery study additionally uses the known injected set to resolve the directional ambiguity of the fixed-\(k\) search, while observation identities remain unavailable to the optimiser. These designs therefore evaluate localisation conditional on an oracle-assisted deletion budget and, where applicable, search direction.

The misspecification study instead evaluates analyst-specified deletion fractions \(k/n\in\{0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}\). The implementation rounds the corresponding sample count to a feasible integer, with \(k/n=0.025\) treated as the primary operating point. Each fraction defines a separate sensitivity budget and generates its own fixed-\(k\) analysis.

The methodology therefore defines influential-set localisation conditional on a prespecified target coefficient and deletion budget. The simulation evidence next examines how the resulting sensitivity structure changes across contamination mechanisms, estimation objectives, and forms of model misspecification.
