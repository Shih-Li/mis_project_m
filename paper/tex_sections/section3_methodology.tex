\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section defines fixed-\(k\) MIS as a coefficient-targeted method for localising joint influence. The methodology isolates a prespecified regression coefficient, expresses its change under set deletion as a set-DFBETA, formulates the fixed-\(k\) subset-selection problem, and exploits its linear-fractional structure for global optimisation. Statistical calibration is treated separately from optimisation.

The deletion size \(k\) is prespecified and defines the sensitivity budget. Different values of \(k\) therefore pose different subset-deletion questions and can generate different influential sets. The simulation designs use either oracle-assisted values of \(k\) or analyst-specified deletion fractions, as described at the end of this section.

\subsection{Regression setup and target coefficient}
\label{subsec:regression_setup}

Consider the linear regression model
\begin{equation}
y_i
=
x_{ij}\beta_j
+
\mathbf{z}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
+
\varepsilon_i,
\qquad i\in[n].
\label{eq:regression_target}
\end{equation}
The coefficient $\beta_j$ is the prespecified target of the influence analysis. Here, $[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ denotes the observation index set, $x_{ij}$ is the scalar target regressor, and $\mathbf{z}_i$ contains the remaining nuisance regressors with coefficient vector $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$. All deletion effects examined in this section therefore refer to changes in the estimate of $\beta_j$ within a fixed regression specification.

The Frisch--Waugh--Lovell theorem isolates the target coefficient by partialling the nuisance regressors out of the target regressor and the outcome \parencite{frisch1933partial,lovell1963seasonal}. Let $\widetilde{x}_i$ and $\widetilde{y}_i$ denote the resulting residualised target regressor and outcome, and write $\widehat{\beta}$ for the OLS estimate of the target coefficient $\beta_j$. The full-sample estimate is then
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\beta}
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{x}_i\widetilde{y}_i}
{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{x}_i^2}.
\label{eq:target_beta_residualised}
\end{equation}
This representation isolates the target coefficient in a scalar regression, allowing the subsequent deletion analysis to use residualised variables rather than the full multivariate specification. 

The search objective uses a fixed-residualised deletion convention. The residualised quantities $\widetilde{x}_i$ and $\widetilde{y}_i$ are computed on the full sample and held fixed when observations are deleted. For a deletion set \(S\subseteq[n]\), define the fixed-residualised leave-set-out coefficient as
\begin{equation}
	\widetilde{\beta}_{-S}
	=
	\frac{
		\sum_{i\notin S}\widetilde{x}_i\widetilde{y}_i
	}{
		\sum_{i\notin S}\widetilde{x}_i^2
	}.
	\label{eq:fixed_residualised_beta}
\end{equation}
The nuisance projection is therefore not re-estimated when computing \(\widetilde{\beta}_{-S}\). The MIS search therefore optimises a fixed-residualised deletion coefficient, and the Dinkelbach procedure applies to this linear-fractional objective \parencite{konrad2026finding}.

Let \(\widehat{\beta}_{-S}\) denote the target coefficient obtained by refitting the original multivariate regression after deleting \(S\). The fixed-residualised coefficient \(\widetilde{\beta}_{-S}\) approximates the coefficient obtained after deletion and full refitting: it holds the full-sample nuisance projection fixed, while \(\widehat{\beta}_{-S}\) recomputes that projection using the retained observations. Frisch--Waugh--Lovell remains exact in both cases; the approximation arises because the MIS search holds the full-sample residualisation fixed. Hence, in general,
\[
\widetilde{\beta}_{-S}\neq\widehat{\beta}_{-S}.
\]
The two coefficients coincide when there are no nuisance regressors and can differ when deletion materially changes the partialling-out projection.

Throughout, \(\widetilde{\beta}_{-S}\) denotes the fixed-residualised search coefficient, while \(\widehat{\beta}_{-S}\) denotes the full-refit deletion coefficient. ``Exact'' refers to global optimisation of the fixed-\(k\), fixed-residualised objective, while \emph{full-refit validation} refers to evaluation using \(\widehat{\beta}_{-S}\).

Define the corresponding residual of the residualised target regression by
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{r}_i
=
\widetilde{y}_i-\widehat{\beta}\widetilde{x}_i.
\label{eq:target_residual}
\end{equation}
These residuals record the variation in the residualised outcome that remains after estimation of the target coefficient, and by Frisch--Waugh--Lovell they coincide with the residuals of the full regression of $y_i$ on $x_{ij}$ and $\mathbf{z}_i$. The difference between the full-sample coefficient \(\widehat{\beta}\) and the fixed-residualised deletion coefficient \(\widetilde{\beta}_{-S}\) measures the coefficient movement used by the MIS search. This difference depends on the deletion set as a whole and therefore defines influence at the level of the set rather than the individual observation.

\subsection{Set influence and set-DFBETA}
\label{subsec:set_influence}

For a deletion set \(S\subseteq[n]\), define the coefficient-targeted set influence by
\begin{equation}
\Delta(S)
=
\widehat{\beta}
-
\widetilde{\beta}_{-S}.
\label{eq:set_dfbeta}
\end{equation}
This quantity is referred to throughout the thesis as the \emph{set-DFBETA}. Its sign records the direction of movement under the fixed-residualised objective: a positive value indicates a lower fixed-residualised deletion coefficient, while a negative value indicates a higher one.

Using the residualised variables defined in \autoref{subsec:regression_setup}, and substituting \autoref{eq:target_beta_residualised} and \autoref{eq:target_residual}, the set-DFBETA admits the closed-form representation
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\beta}
-
\widetilde{\beta}_{-S}
=
\frac{
	\sum_{s\in S}\widetilde{x}_s\widetilde{r}_s
}{
	\sum_{t\notin S}\widetilde{x}_t^2
}.
\label{eq:set_dfbeta_ratio}
\end{equation}
The numerator collects the score contributions of the deleted observations. The denominator records the residualised design information remaining after deletion \parencite{konrad2026finding}. Set influence therefore depends jointly on the contribution removed from the sample and on the information retained outside the deletion set.

Set influence is a joint property of the selected observations. Changing the membership of $S$ alters the numerator and the denominator of \autoref{eq:set_dfbeta_ratio} at the same time. Each deleted observation also removes design information from the denominator. The set-DFBETA is therefore not generally equal to the sum of the corresponding single-observation deletion effects. When score contributions have the same sign, joint deletion can amplify their combined effect, as formalised in \autoref{lem:superadditive}. This dependence can contribute to masking and motivates searching for influential sets jointly rather than relying solely on observation-level rankings. The following subsection formalises that selection problem for a fixed set size $k$.

\subsection{Fixed-\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} most influential sets}
\label{subsec:fixed_k_mis}

For a prespecified deletion size \(k<n\), the fixed-\(k\) MIS is defined by
\begin{equation}
S_k^*
\in
\arg\max_{\substack{S\subseteq[n]\\|S|=k}}
|\Delta(S)|.
\label{eq:absolute_fixed_k_influence}
\end{equation}
The absolute value allows influential movement in either coefficient direction. Computationally, the two directional problems can be solved separately and compared after optimisation.

Each prespecified value of \(k\) defines a separate deletion-budget question and can produce a different maximising set. In particular, the maximisers need not be nested: nothing in \autoref{eq:absolute_fixed_k_influence} requires \(S_k^\ast\subset S_{k+1}^\ast\), because the optimal size-\((k+1)\) set is chosen afresh rather than by extending the optimal size-\(k\) set. Evaluating a sequence of values therefore traces how target-coefficient sensitivity and influential-set membership change with the deletion budget. The analysis does not treat \(k\) as an estimated contamination count.

Direct enumeration requires evaluation of \(\binom{n}{k}\) subsets. The closed-form set-DFBETA provides additional structure: its numerator sums the deleted observations' score contributions, while its denominator subtracts their design contributions from the full-sample total. This structure yields the linear-fractional optimisation problem used below.

\subsection{Linear-fractional reduction and Dinkelbach optimisation}
\label{subsec:dinkelbach_optimisation}

The fixed-residualised representation in \autoref{eq:set_dfbeta_ratio} allows the fixed-$k$ influence problem to be written in a compact linear-fractional form. Define
\begin{equation}
w_i := \widetilde{x}_i\widetilde{r}_i,
\qquad
c_i := \widetilde{x}_i^2,
\qquad
T := \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i,
\label{eq:linear_fractional_components}
\end{equation}
and, for a deletion set $S$,
\begin{equation}
W(S) := \sum_{i\in S} w_i,
\qquad
G(S) := T-\sum_{i\in S} c_i.
\label{eq:WG_definitions}
\end{equation}
Here, $W(S)$ collects the deleted score contributions, while $G(S)$ measures the residualised design information remaining after deletion. The set-DFBETA in \autoref{eq:set_dfbeta_ratio} can therefore be written as
\begin{equation}
\Delta(S)
=
\frac{W(S)}{G(S)}.
\label{eq:linear_fractional_objective}
\end{equation}
The fixed-$k$ influence objective is therefore a ratio of two quantities that are additive in set membership.

The ratio in \autoref{eq:linear_fractional_objective} requires a positive denominator for every admissible deletion set. For a fixed set size $k$, the analysis therefore assumes

\begin{equation}
G(S)>0
\qquad
\text{for all } S\subseteq[n] \text{ such that } |S|=k.
\label{eq:positive_denominator}
\end{equation}
Under this condition, deleting any admissible size-\(k\) set leaves positive residualised information for estimating the target coefficient. For the positive direction, the fixed-\(k\) problem can be expressed as the linear-fractional optimisation problem.

\begin{equation}
\max_{\substack{S\subseteq[n]\\|S|=k}}
\frac{W(S)}{G(S)}.
\label{eq:fixed_k_fractional_problem}
\end{equation}
The negative-direction problem is obtained by replacing \(W(S)\) with \(-W(S)\). The absolute-value MIS in \autoref{eq:absolute_fixed_k_influence} compares the optimised solutions from the two directions. This formulation preserves the joint dependence of the numerator and denominator on the selected deletion set \parencite{konrad2026finding}.

Under the positive-denominator condition, each directional fixed-\(k\) problem is a linear-fractional optimisation problem. For the positive direction and a Dinkelbach parameter \(\eta\), the transformed objective ranks observations by
\[
s_i(\eta)=w_i+\eta c_i.
\]
For a fixed \(\eta\), selecting the \(k\) largest transformed scores maximises the corresponding Dinkelbach objective, reducing the subset-selection step to a top-\(k\) ranking. The ratio is then updated using \(\eta=W(S)/G(S)\), and the top-\(k\) problem is resolved. Under exact arithmetic and exact top-\(k\) selection, the sequence terminates finitely \parencite{dinkelbach1967nonlinear,konrad2026finding}.

For fixed residualised inputs and the positive-denominator condition in \autoref{eq:positive_denominator}, this procedure identifies a global maximiser of each directional fixed-\(k\) linear-fractional objective. Comparing the two directional optima therefore yields a global maximiser of the absolute fixed-\(k\) objective in \autoref{eq:absolute_fixed_k_influence}. This result provides the ``exact'' optimisation terminology defined in \autoref{subsec:regression_setup}. Numerical convergence and computational checks are reported in \autoref{app:numerical}.

\subsection{Optimisation and calibration}
\label{subsec:evt_fixed_k}

Optimisation and statistical calibration answer separate questions. Optimisation identifies the set that maximises the fixed-\(k\) objective, while calibration asks whether the attained influence is larger than a reference model would produce. Because the maximum is taken over \(\binom{n}{k}\) overlapping candidate sets, its reference distribution differs from that of a prespecified set, and the attained value cannot be read as though the deletion set had been fixed in advance.

The conclusions of this thesis do not rest on a formal test of the selected set. The contamination simulations evaluate localisation against the known injected set, and the empirical audits report deletion paths. Where calibration is required, in the misspecification study, the reference distribution of the standardised deletion statistic is obtained from independent null simulations (\autoref{subsec:misspecification}). A block-maxima extreme-value construction \parencite{konrad2026testing} was also evaluated as a candidate calibration device; \autoref{app:evt} documents why it is not used for inference here.

\subsection{Role of \texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} in the simulation designs}
\label{subsec:k_simulation_role}

The simulations use \(k\) in two distinct ways. In the distributional-recovery and robust-estimation studies, the data-generating process supplies an oracle deletion budget equal to the injected-set size. The distributional-recovery study additionally uses the known injected set to resolve the directional ambiguity of the fixed-\(k\) search, while observation identities remain unavailable to the optimiser. These designs therefore evaluate localisation conditional on an oracle-assisted deletion budget and, where applicable, search direction.

The misspecification study instead evaluates analyst-specified deletion fractions \(k/n\in\{0.01,0.025,0.05,0.10\}\). The implementation rounds the corresponding sample count to a feasible integer, with \(k/n=0.025\) treated as the primary operating point. Each fraction defines a separate sensitivity budget and generates its own fixed-\(k\) analysis.

The methodology therefore defines influential-set localisation conditional on a prespecified target coefficient and deletion budget. The simulation evidence next examines how the resulting sensitivity structure changes across contamination mechanisms, estimation objectives, and forms of model misspecification.
